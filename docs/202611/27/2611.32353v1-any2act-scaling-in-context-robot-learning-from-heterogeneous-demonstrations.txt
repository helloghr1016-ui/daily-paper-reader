## Abstract 

Robot in-context learning (ICL) enables a policy to per-form unseen tasks by following contextual demonstrations, without task-specific fine-tuning. This is increasingly valu-able as large language models reasoning at low frequency can specify tasks through demonstrations and delegate high-frequency execution to such a policy. However, methods conditioned on human videos are bounded by scarce human– robot pairs, while those conditioned on robot demonstrations accept only one or two source types at limited scale. We present Any2Act , a foundation model for robot ICL. Our key insight is that a demonstration should be expressed in the camera layout and action space of the robot that performs it (the executor ), not of whoever provided it (the demon-strator ). This unified embodiment-grounded representation decouples learning to follow from admitting a new source. Any2Act learns to follow once, from robot-to-robot pairs that already take this form in 1.2M real trajectories spanning 10K tasks. Human videos, simulation, UMI, and other robots are then translated into this representation, and source-routed adapters trained on only a few hundred pairs each absorb the residual gap while the pretrained model stays frozen. For long horizons, a training-free tracker feeds only the demon-stration window around the executor’s progress, bounding the context and rolling back to redo failed steps. From a single demonstration, Any2Act achieves 57.5% success on unseen tasks without fine-tuning, exceeding VLAs fine-tuned on 50 demonstrations per task; other demonstrators retain 74–91% of the success achieved with the executor’s own demonstrations. 

1arXiv:2611.32353v1 [cs.RO] 24 Nov 2026 1. Introduction 

Humans can acquire new skills simply by watching video demonstrations, a capability that robot learning has long aspired to. Toward this goal, robot in-context learning (ICL) has recently emerged [3, 7, 9, 16, 41, 49]: conditioned on a few contextual examples, such as robot trajectories or hu-man videos, a policy can directly perform unseen tasks that are semantically consistent with the given context, without any task-specific fine-tuning. This capability becomes even more important as large language models (LLMs) acquire stronger embodied reasoning abilities [11, 15, 23]. Since such models typically reason and plan at a low frequency, a promising paradigm is to invoke an in-context policy as a 

tool call : an LLM agent specifies the task through diverse demonstrations, and the policy executes it at a high control frequency. Serving in this role places stringent demands on the policy’s generalizability and scalability. However, existing ICL methods remain far from meet-ing these demands, facing three key challenges. The first is 

scalable supervision for demonstration following. Meth-ods conditioned on human videos must learn from task-aligned human–robot pairs [3, 16, 49], which remain scarce, and the videos themselves lack action labels and often hide hand–object contact behind the hand. Methods conditioned on robot demonstrations avoid such pairing, but are trained on synthetic or benchmark-scale data [6, 31, 33, 41], which limits the range of tasks they can follow. The second is 

demonstration heterogeneity . Each prior method accepts only one or two types of demonstration. In real-world deploy-ment, however, a policy must handle not only diverse scenes and tasks but also heterogeneous demonstrations, such as human videos, simulation rollouts, UMI recordings [5], and trajectories from robots of different embodiments, and admit-ting a new type should not require a new large-scale paired dataset and retraining. The third is generalizable demon-stration following . A demonstration specifies not only what to do but also how, such as the grasp, the order of steps, and the bimanual coordination. Following it thus requires more than replay: the policy must transfer this motion to new objects and scenes, and, when execution deviates or fails, retry and resume over long horizons. In this work, we present Any2Act (Fig. 1), a foundation model for robot in-context learning that addresses these chal-lenges. We call the robot performing the task the executor ,and whatever provides the demonstration (a teleoperated robot, simulator, UMI, human, or another robot) the demon-strator . Our key insight is to express a demonstration in the executor’s camera layout and action space rather than the demonstrator’s; we term this a unified embodiment-grounded representation . A single policy can then follow demonstra-tions from any source, with lightweight adapters bridging the residual gap in appearance and motion. This decouples learning demonstration following, which needs large-scale paired data, from admitting a new source, which needs only a translation pipeline and a few hundred pairs. To learn demonstration following at scale, we pretrain Any2Act on robot-to-robot demonstration pairs. Teleoper-ated collection records each task many times, and any two recordings of the same task form a pair with accurate ac-tions and wrist and head views. Our corpus comprises 1.2M real trajectories (10K hours) spanning 10K tasks. No other demonstrator is involved at this stage: the following ability is learned once, in the executor’s own space, and is shared by every source later translated into it. To handle demonstration heterogeneity, we admit new sources in two steps. First, every source is translated into the unified embodiment-grounded representation. For example, an agentic ego2sim pipeline re-enacts a human video on a simulated executor, completing the wrist views and recov-ering the actions that egocentric videos lack. Second, to absorb the residual gap in appearance and motion without compromising the foundation model, we attach a plug-in source-routed adapter to each source, activated only on its translated yet still imperfect demonstration tokens. We show that a few hundred paired demonstrations suffice to adapt to a new source, while the base model’s behavior remains unchanged. Paired data are thus needed only at this small scale per source, rather than to learn following itself. For generalizable demonstration following, Any2Act reads not the whole demonstration as global context but a window around the executor’s current progress, located by a training-free tracker, keeping the context focused and bounded over long horizons. This window also enables re-covery: a failure such as a missed grasp or a slipped object often returns the scene close to an earlier phase, so the tracker rolls the window back to redo the failed step. In real-world experiments, Any2Act performs unseen tasks with 57.5% success without fine-tuning, exceeding VLAs fine-tuned on 50 demonstrations per task, while prior in-context policies reach at most 19.7%. Demonstrations from simulation, human videos, UMI, and other robots re-tain 74–91% of the success achieved with the executor’s own demonstrations, each through adapters trained on only 200 pairs. We contribute: (1) Any2Act, a foundation model for demonstration following, pretrained at scale; (2) a unified embodiment-grounded representation with source-routed adapters, which admits heterogeneous demonstrations with-out changing the pretrained model; and (3) a training-free progress tracker that bounds the context over long horizons and enables failure recovery.

2.2. In-Context Learning for Robot Manipulation 

Demonstrations can specify spatial and temporal details that language leaves ambiguous. Robot in-context learning uses them to guide execution without task-specific parameter up-dates [7, 14, 16, 26]. Recent industrial systems such as GEN-1.5 [12] and Skild S1 [38] perform new tasks from a single demonstration, highlighting the potential of this paradigm. Tab. 1 compares recent open-source policies along three axes. 

Training data: prior methods rely on synthetic pairs [33], benchmark imitation data [6, 31], human–robot pairs [3], or generated human videos [49]; Any2Act is pretrained on robot-to-robot pairs drawn from 1.2M real trajectories, which are collected naturally from routine data collection with accu-rate actions. Demonstration sources: each prior method ac-cepts one or two; Any2Act is the only one that admits all five, including simulation and cross-embodiment robots, through a unified embodiment-grounded representation. Condition-ing: prior methods encode the whole demonstration as global context [6, 22, 31, 33] or a full prefix [49], or track progress with a learned aligner, without demonstration states and roll-back [3]; Any2Act pairs a training-free tracker that rolls back after failures with dense demonstration states, keeping the context bounded while conveying the motion.

Our agentic ego2sim pipeline (Sec. UMI recordings and other robots’ data already contain a head view and two wrist views, so only their motion needs to be mapped into the executor’s action space, from end-effector poses for UMI and joint layouts for other robots. Translated demonstrations share the executor’s format but not its distribution: UMI frames still show a handheld gripper, simulated rollouts carry rendering artifacts and inac-curate physics, and converted motion inherits tracking and kinematic errors. We absorb these residual differences with source-routed adapters (Sec. 3.5). 

3.2. Progress Tracking and Recovery 

Once translated, a demonstration is followed step by step, so encoding it in full would make the context grow with its length. The policy instead reads only a short window around the executor’s current progress. Placing this window requires knowing which demonstration step the executor has reached, which is hard in the real world: at deployment only the past is observed; the executor moves at a different speed, pauses, 4and deviates; and demonstrations come from other bodies and unseen tasks. Rather than a learned aligner [3], which would need retraining for every source, we split alignment in two: a rough, training-free tracker only places the current moment inside the window, and the policy, trained to tolerate its errors, pinpoints its progress within it. Concretely, at each execution step t the tracker finds an anchor, the demonstration step that best matches the execu-tor’s current observation, using frozen DINOv3 [37] fea-tures f . Besides the head view, we match the wrist views, which add the gripper–object relation that the head view often misses [35], especially across sources (Sec. 4.6). Because the policy reads only the window, the anchor acts as its pointer into the demonstration, which also enables recovery. The policy itself absorbs small deviations, such as a displaced object, since pretraining pairs already differ in layout. A failure, however, often returns the scene to a state the demonstration has already shown: after a missed grasp, the object stays around the original place; after a slip, it lies back on the table. Since the monotonic tracker does not move back by itself, we re-localize the anchor to an earlier phase when it explains the current observation clearly better (details in Sec. B.3), so the robot redoes the failed step without recovery data or additional training. 

3.3. Model Architecture 

Given the demonstration window and the current observa-tion, Any2Act predicts how the scene should evolve and the actions that make it so (Fig. 3). This follows world action models [46, 50], which predict future video alongside actions so that the priors of video pretraining carry over to action generation. In context, this prediction is harder and more important: the future must follow the demonstration while fitting a current scene with a different layout and objects. We therefore build Any2Act on the pretrained video model Cosmos-Predict2 [27] and cast demonstration following as conditional continuation. The demonstration window is given in two complemen-tary forms: sparse visual tokens, encoded from a few frames of the three views to convey the scene, and dense state tokens, summarizing the proprioception over the window to convey motion and timing. The executor is given likewise, by its current views and its proprioceptive history; the language instruction enters through cross-attention. Figure S1 details the token layout. Two streams produce the two outputs. The video model places the demonstration tokens and the current views in one sequence, so that the two can be compared directly, and predicts the future frame. A lightweight action expert takes the executor’s proprioceptive history and the demonstration state tokens, reads the video model’s features through cross-attention, and predicts the action chunk. The two outputs are generated jointly to stay consistent. At deployment, demonstration latents are precomputed and the context is encoded once per control step and reused across denoising steps, yielding a two-second 30 Hz action chunk in 180 ms on one H200 GPU (Sec. A.5). 

3.4. Demonstration-Following Pretraining 

Learning to follow demonstrations at all requires numer-ous demonstration-following pairs. Human–robot pairs with accurate action labels are scarce, so prior work resorts to synthetic human videos [49] or a few thousand real pairs [3]. Our unified representation removes the need for such pairs: the policy only needs to learn to follow demonstrations within it, and robot-to-robot pairs already come in this form, with Tk the identity. Such pairs are also easy to obtain at scale: teleoperated data collection usually records each task many times, and any two recordings of the same task form a pair with accurate actions and all three views. Our corpus contains 1.2M trajectories over 10K hours in 10K tasks (Fig. 2), labeled and filtered by a VLM [34] and grouped by task. Recordings of the same task span dif-ferent environments, object instances, and placements, so the policy must carry the demonstrated behavior over to the present scene rather than replay it. 3.5. Source-Routed Adapters for New Sources 

With θ pretrained on robot pairs, a demonstration trans-lated from a new source already arrives in the executor’s format, yet its residual differences in appearance and motion (Sec. 3.1) change how the policy should interpret it. Fine-tuning θ directly is risky: paired data for a new source are scarce, and fully fine-tuning on so few pairs risks overfitting and degrading the demonstration-following ability learned from robot data (Tab. S9). We therefore keep θ frozen and propose source-routed adapters (SRAs), which adapt only how the policy interprets each source’s demonstrations, us-ing a few hundred pairs per source. Each SRA is a low-rank residual branch [13] attached to the frozen model and acti-vated only on the demonstration tokens of its source k:

h′ = W0h + mk BkAkh, (3) where h is a token feature, W0 is a frozen linear layer, Ak

and Bk are the low-rank factors of source k at that layer, which over all layers form ϕk, and mk ∈ { 0, 1} indicates whether the token belongs to a demonstration of source k.Adapters are inserted into layers of both the video model and the action expert, covering the visual and the state tokens of the demonstration. For other robots, whose kinematics also differ, an additional embodiment adapter is trained on the same pairs (Sec. C.2). Demonstrations recorded on the executor itself pass through no adapter, so following them behaves exactly as before adaptation. Adapters of different sources act on different demonstrations and can thus be attached, removed, or combined without retraining. We show that each new source needs only a few hundred pairs to gain the ability to follow its demonstrations (Sec. 4.5).

4.1. Comparison Protocol 

Comparing in-context policies with prior methods is subtle, because they differ in what they receive at test time and in what they must be trained on beforehand. Language-conditioned VLAs need no demonstration at inference, but acquire a new task only through fine-tuning on data from the same environment; we therefore fine-tune them on every evaluation task. In-context policies need no task-specific fine-tuning in principle, but their generalization is bounded by the tasks, skills, visual inputs, and embodiment they were trained on, so their released checkpoints cannot be evaluated directly on our robot. We therefore retrain them with their own methods on all of our robot pairs, and evaluate them with their required demonstrations and no fine-tuning. Some settings, however, lie beyond what any pretraining corpus covers: a simulator differs from the real world in appear-ance and physics, and some tasks require skills or object properties absent from pretraining, such as deformable or articulated objects. A single demonstration specifies what to do but cannot supply such a skill. In these settings, all in-context policies, including ours, are first adapted by su-pervised fine-tuning on a small in-domain set, and are still conditioned on a demonstration at test time. 

4.2. Evaluating in the Real World 

Platform and benchmark. All real-world experiments run on a Galbot G1 [10] dual-arm mobile robot with a head camera and two wrist cameras. We construct two bench-marks of six tasks each (Fig. 4; descriptions and success criteria in Tab. S3), none of which appears in pretraining. Both span a range of difficulty; they differ in whether the required skills are covered by pretraining. The Task Gen-eralization benchmark tests whether a policy can perform a new task from a demonstration alone: its tasks compose skills largely seen in pretraining into new goals, step or-ders, and object combinations, and Any2Act executes them without fine-tuning. The Skill Extension benchmark tests whether a policy can acquire skills beyond pretraining, such as handling deformable objects or atomic actions never seen before; here every in-context policy is first adapted as in Sec. 4.1. In both benchmarks, object instances, layouts, and backgrounds differ substantially from those in pretraining, adaptation, and the given demonstration, so the policy must transfer the demonstrated behavior to a new scene. 

Baselines. We compare with two language-conditioned VLAs, π0.5 [32] and GR00T N1.7 [28, 29], and two in-context policies, HOST [3] and Zero-WAM [49]. Since the policy is trained with perturbed anchors (Sec. 3.2), the tracker only needs to place the correct demonstration content inside the window. We thus measure window cov-erage : annotators mark the matching demonstration time of each execution frame, and we report how often it falls inside the window tracked online as deployed. We annotate 1,000 pairs spanning multiple demonstration sources and tasks (annotation protocol in Sec. F.8). On same-embodiment pairs, our training-free tracker matches HOST’s learned aligner retrained on our robot pairs. On other sources HOST degrades sharply, to 42.8 on cross-embodiment robots, while ours stays 8–13 points higher. Wrist views improve coverage on every source, by up to 12.0 points on UMI. Coverage follows the source order of success in Table 5, suggesting that locating progress is a major part of the remaining cost of a new source. ## 5. Conclusion 

We presented Any2Act, a world action model for robot in-context learning that follows demonstrations from human videos, simulation, UMI recordings, and other robots by translating them into a unified embodiment-grounded repre-sentation. Pretrained on robot pairs from 1.2M real trajecto-ries, it performs unseen tasks from a single demonstration; source-routed adapters admit new sources without altering it, and a training-free tracker keeps the context bounded and rolls back to redo failed steps. Limitations, future directions, and broader impact are discussed in Sec. G.

## A. Model Details 

This section specifies what the policy reads and how it is computed; Fig. S1 summarizes the token layout. 

A.1. Executor Representation 

Views. The executor, a Galbot G1 [10], carries a head camera and two wrist cameras. Head images are resized to 320 ×320 and wrist images to 224 ×224 . A translated demonstration is stored in exactly this format. 

State and action space. Every state and action is a 19-D vector at 30 Hz: seven joint angles per arm, one opening per gripper (0 closed, 1 open), and the mobile-base pose 

(x, y, ψ ). The base pose is always expressed relative to the body frame at a reference time (the current time for the executor, the window start for the demonstration), so that the policy never sees absolute odometry and a demonstration recorded elsewhere in a room is directly comparable. 

Prediction. An action is a chunk of H=60 absolute states covering the next 2 s; the model jointly predicts the three camera frames 2 s ahead, i.e . h=60 in Eq. (1). 

A.2. Demonstration Window and Tokens 

Window. Given an anchor ˆτ from the tracker (Sec. B), the window samples five images at the nominal times ˆτ +

γ · {− 1, 0, 1, 2, 3} s, each snapped to the nearest recorded frame, i.e . Wp=γ and Wf =3 γ in Sec. 3.2, where γ is a speed factor. At deployment γ=1 ; during training γ is drawn log-uniformly from [0 .8, 1.25] to cover speed differences between demonstrator and executor. Near the start or end of a demonstration the grid is shifted inside the episode instead of being truncated, so the window always holds five real frames when the demonstration is long enough. 

Visual tokens. The five frames of each camera are encoded by the frozen causal Wan VAE of Cosmos-Predict2 [27], which compresses time by a factor of four and encodes the first frame on its own, so that 1+4 n frames give 1+ n latents. Five frames thus give the two demonstration latents d0 (first frame) and d1 (remaining four) per camera (Fig. S1a). Each latent is placed on the temporal axis of the rotary embedding at its time in the window; the current and predicted frames sit at 0 and +2 s. 

State tokens. The demonstrator’s 30 Hz states inside the window, expressed in the executor’s state space, (at most 151 rows) are split into 12 equal time bins. Each bin yields a pose 

token (the per-channel mean; grippers take their last value) and a motion token (the change over the bin divided by its duration; grippers take their open/close change), giving 24 tokens that convey timing and motion far more densely than the two visual latents. The executor contributes its 16 most recent states. 

A.3. Video Model and Visibility Rule 

The video model is the 2B-parameter DiT of Cosmos-Predict2 [27] (28 blocks, width 2048). Its sequence concate-nates, for each of the three cameras, the patchified latents 

[d0, d 1, current , future ], followed by the 24 demonstration-state tokens (Fig. S1b). Learned embeddings mark the role of a token (demonstration or query), its camera, and, for demonstration tokens, their time; state tokens are embedded per hand (seven joints and gripper) and for the base. All demonstration tokens pass through a gated attention block, the DemoAdapter , which is part of the pretrained weights 

θ and distinct from the source-routed adapters of Sec. C. The language instruction enters every block through cross-attention to frozen T5 embeddings. 

Visibility rule. Demonstration, demonstration-state and current tokens are clean : they attend only to clean tokens; the noisy future attends to all tokens (Fig. S1c). A.4. Action Expert and Objective 

The action expert is a 16-layer transformer (width 1536, 12 heads, ≈0.5B parameters) over the sequence [16 executor-history tokens, 24 demonstration-state tokens, 60 noisy ac-tion tokens]. Every fourth layer cross-attends to the hidden states of the video model after blocks 7, 14, 21 and 28, so it reads both low-level geometry and the model’s anticipation of the future. The demonstration-state tokens are shared with the video model and give the expert direct access to the demonstrated motion. 

Objective. Both outputs are trained with rectified flow. For the action chunk a with noise ϵ and t ∼ U [0 , 1] , the network predicts the velocity ϵ − a from (1 − t)a + tϵ; the squared error weighs the first second of the chunk by 1, the second by 0.5, and the gripper channels twice. The video term follows Cosmos-Predict2 (log-normal σ with log σ ∼ N (0 , 1) and EDM weighting [17]) and is averaged over the three cameras. The total loss is L = 2 Laction + Lvideo , with the action term normalized over the global batch. 

A.5. Inference and Latency 

At each control step the tracker updates the anchor, the win-dow is assembled, and actions and future frames are sampled 

jointly : a Karras schedule ( σ ∈ [0 .002 , 80] , ρ=7 ) with a second-order RES multistep update for the video and Euler steps for the actions. We use 4 denoising steps. The robot executes the first second (30 rows) of each 2 s chunk before the next chunk is applied, so the policy re-plans at 1 Hz while every executed action remains conditioned on a two-second look-ahead. Three caches follow from the visibility rule. The VAE la-tents of the demonstration window are kept on the GPU and reused across chunks while the window does not change; the keys and values of the clean tokens (demonstration, demon-stration state, current views) are computed once per observa-tion and reused across the denoising steps; the cross-attention keys and values of the instruction are computed once per demonstration. Each row is the median of 50 timed calls after warm-up, covering preprocessing, encoding of the current views, denoising and the transfer of the actions to the CPU. The tracker update (DINOv3 on the current and previous frame of the three views, DTW update and rollback test) adds 19.4 ms per chunk. B.2. Training Anchors and Perturbation 

Training anchors. During training the whole query record-ing is in view, so Eq. (2) is evaluated in both directions and the anchor τt = arg min s[Ft(s)+ Bt(s)] lies on the globally optimal path. We evaluate it once per pair, offline, when the pair is built (Sec. D.2). Since the path is optimized glob-ally rather than estimated greedily from the past, this offline evaluation does not need the step limit and penalties that stabilize the deployed tracker, and its matching cost can also compare the gripper events of the two recordings, which are both known in advance; Sec. D.2 gives the exact cost. 

Perturbation. The window is anchored at the perturbed anchor τt + ϵ. The deviation ϵ is drawn from N (0 , 0.4 s),which spans the typical deviation of the deployed tracker, and truncated to [−2.9γ, 0.9γ] s so that τt stays inside the window [τt + ϵ − γ, τ t + ϵ + 3 γ]. With probability 0.2 it is instead drawn far behind ( [−2.9γ, −2.6γ] s) or far ahead ([0 .6γ, 0.9γ] s), the cases that a lagging or overshooting tracker produces. The window is randomly stretched or compressed in time by the speed factor γ (Sec. A). 

Consequence for the tracker. Because the policy learns to locate its own progress inside the window, the deployed tracker only needs coverage : the correct demonstration time must fall inside the window. Since the window extends 3γ

ahead of the anchor and γ behind it, it tolerates an anchor that lags the true progress by up to 3 s but leads it by at most 1 s. Table 6 reports this coverage for every source (Sec. F.8). 

B.3. Rollback 

The monotone tracker cannot move back by itself, yet a fail-ure often returns the scene to a phase the demonstration has already shown. Section 3.2 therefore re-localizes the anchor to an earlier phase when it explains the current observation clearly better. Below, “clearly better” is made precise by a 14 gain and a margin, measured with a cost that is sensitive to the task-relevant part of the scene. 

Motion-weighted dense cost. For each reference we build a patch-weighting that concentrates on what changes in the demonstration. On the head view, 80% of the weight is spread over the patches whose features move within ±2 sof the reference (above the 85th percentile, dilated by one patch) and 20% is uniform. On each wrist view, the weight is proportional to how much the corresponding arm moves within ±0.5 s; an arm with less than 15% of the motion is dropped, and the bottom quarter of the wrist image, which shows mostly the gripper itself, is down-weighted to 0.25. The dense cost compares the weighted patch features of the current and the 0.1 s earlier frame with each reference, which suppresses single-frame noise. 

Candidates. The dense cost is minimized over the ref-erences from Lrb before the current anchor up to the next grasp of the demonstration that the executor has not yet performed. Grasps are counted from the executor’s gripper and re-synchronized with the anchor, so this upper bound always lies after the anchor; its role is to keep phases be-yond an unperformed grasp, which may look similar to the present scene, out of the comparison. Let τ ∗ be the mini-mizer. A rollback is considered only if τ ∗ lies between 1 s and Lrb before the previous anchor ˆτt−1. The look-back length Lrb =15 s bounds how far back a failure may set the task.

C.1. Placement and Routing 

Each branch in Eq. (3) has rank 32; we use the scaling α=32 ,so α/r =1 and the update is exactly mkBkAkh. In the video model, branches are attached to the query, key, value and output projections of every self-attention and to both linear layers of every feed-forward block; in the action expert, to the same layers of every block, plus a rank-16 branch on the projection of the demonstration-state tokens. Together these form ϕk, with ≈47.2M trainable parameters per source, about 1.9% of the 2.46B-parameter policy. The routing mask mk is 1 exactly for the tokens of a demonstration from source k: its visual latents and its state tokens, in both the video model and the action expert, as in Sec. 3.5. Current-observation, language, history, future and action tokens never pass through a branch. Cross-attention layers are left untouched, so the action expert reads the adapted demonstration only through the video model’s fea-tures and its own adapted state tokens. 

Exactness. Because every branch is multiplied by mk, a demonstration recorded on the executor ( mk=0 ) produces exactly the same activations with or without the adapter, so following it behaves exactly as before adaptation. Section F.7 compares this with full fine-tuning on the same pairs. 

C.2. Embodiment Adapter 

For another robot, joint angles do not mean the same thing as the executor’s even after reordering into the shared 19-D layout. The embodiment adapter of Sec. 3.5 is a residual MLP ( 14 →128 →128 →14 , SiLU, last layer zero-initialized; 20K parameters) that maps the 14 arm joints of a routed demonstration toward the executor’s joint space before the state tokens are pooled. Being zero-initialized, it starts as the identity. It is trained jointly with the source’s SRA on the same pairs and is the only component that differs between robot and non-robot sources. 15 C.3. Training 

As in Sec. 4.5, each adapter is trained for 500 steps on 200 paired trajectories from 20 tasks that are disjoint from the 12 evaluation tasks, with θ frozen: 10 source demonstrations per task, each paired with one executor recording of the same task in a different scene. ## D. Pretraining Data and Recipe 

D.1. Corpus 

The corpus consists of 1.2M teleoperated trajectories (10K hours, 30 s on average) recorded on Galbot G1 robots during routine data collection, in various environments (homes, of-fices, retail stores, kitchens and laboratories). Each recording is labeled by a VLM (Qwen3.8 [34]) with a canonical instruc-tion and a task identity. Recordings that share a task identity form a task group; the 10K groups follow a long-tailed size distribution and the task-type composition of Fig. 2. No recording of the twelve evaluation tasks, in any scene, is part of the corpus (Sec. F.1). 

D.2. Pair Construction

Candidates. Two recordings form a candidate pair if they share task group and have the same gripper signature : the same initial gripper states and the same ordered sequence of grasp and release events (interleaved over both arms). E.1. Assets and Physics 

The asset library (Fig. S4) contains hundreds of registered assets (objects, robots and fixtures), many of them instances of shared parametric templates. Frequently used classes can be called by name, with resting poses, joint definitions, annotated grasp candidates and operation points on lids, doors and knobs. 

Rigid and articulated assets. An asset is built from cali-brated robot views of the real object. An LLM describes its parts, joints and openings, citing the frames that show them and marking what cannot be seen ( e.g . wall thickness) as unknown. The description is compiled into a short shape pro-gram (revolved, lofted and swept profiles, boxes and hinges) that produces the visual and the collision mesh from the same parameters. The program is then scaled to metric size using the calibrated cameras, silhouettes and the table plane. Before use, we check each asset for manipulation: openings must be clear, cavities connected, handles must leave room for the fingers (probe points in Fig. S4), and joints must have the observed range. A person then inspects the asset and refines it where needed. 

Bags and liquids. Cloth, paper and open bags are trian-gle shells simulated with position-based dynamics (PBD) in Genesis, and filled pouches use implicit FEM. For pouring, the source container holds a finite amount of liquid; when it is tilted over the target, particles are emitted at the spout and their free flow is simulated with DFSPH. Material param-eters (stiffness, density, viscosity) are nominal values, not measurements. 

E.2. Human Videos via ego2sim 

Our agentic ego2sim builds on EgoEngine [24], which recon-structs egocentric hand–object interaction in simulation. As in Sec. 3.1, it estimates hand and object poses from the video, rebuilds the scene, transfers the grasp to the robot gripper and executes the human trajectory on the simulated executor (Fig. S5). A human hand and a two-finger gripper cannot follow the same path, so the trajectory is transferred at the level of the objects: ego2sim keeps what the human does to the objects , namely which object is touched, at which part, by which hand, in which order and to which end state, and solves again how the robot achieves it. For example, when a person grasps a screwdriver by its handle, puts it into a case and closes the lid, ego2sim keeps three facts: the contact is on the handle, the tool ends up fully inside the case, and the lid is then closed. The grasp, the path over the case wall and the arm pose for pushing the lid are solved for the robot.

A reviewer checks the draft against the video, especially contacts hidden by the hand, which takes 3.5 min per video on average. E.3. Simulation via an LLM Agent 

When no human video of a task is available, a demonstration can still be produced from the instruction alone. Following recent real-to-sim pipelines [20, 48], we model the current demonstration scene from one head-camera frame, and the LLM agent GPT-6 Astra [11] rolls out the task on the simu-lated executor, generating its actions directly, as in Sec. 3.1 (Fig. S6). The agent is called twice, once to recognize the scene and once to plan. It gives only a rough layout and an ordered plan; heights and contacts are computed by the simulator, and trajectories by a local motion planner [44, 45], which also lets failed attempts be retried freely. 

(1) Input. A head-camera frame of the current demonstra-tion scene and the instruction. 

(2) Matching. The first call receives the frame, the instruc-tion and the catalog of callable assets (Sec. E.1). For every visible object it returns the asset id, an approximate position on the table, the yaw, a discrete resting pose, the initial joint state, e.g . an open case lid, and support relations such as “on the case”. The resting pose matters: a pack of wipes standing on its side cannot be obtained from a lying one by a yaw rotation. 

(3) Assembly. The scene is built in MuJoCo [40]. Heights follow from collision geometry and support relations, so an object on top of the case is placed on the case’s top surface. The resulting poses, hinge axes and annotated grasp and operation points are passed on to the planner. 

(4) Planning. The second call receives these ac-tual coordinates and writes an ordered list of primi-tives ( place in , place on , open lid , close lid ,

open door , close door , pour , handover , brace )for the two arms of the executor, each with an arm, an object, a target and, where needed, a sub-goal such as “fully inside” before a lid is closed. Each primitive comes with its own success checks (contained, supported, upright, joint angle). 

(5) Execution. A local controller picks a feasible annotated grasp, computes a collision-free joint trajectory for the sim-ulated executor and executes it in MuJoCo. A grasp holds the object only after both finger pads have touched it from opposite sides for 30 ms, so the simulator cannot carry an object the gripper did not reach. When a primitive fails, the controller first tries other grasps or trajectories on a copy of the current state, and only then is the planner asked to replace the failed primitive, without changing the other primitives or the goal. A verified demonstration takes about 1.2 min. 

E.4. UMI 

UMI recordings [5] already contain a head view and two wrist views, but their motion is a 6-DoF gripper pose and a gripper width. We express the poses in the executor’s base frame with a fixed calibration between the UMI head rig and the executor’s head camera, solve IK on the executor’s arms with the solver of Sec. E.2, and map the width linearly to the executor’s gripper opening. The views are kept as recorded; the handheld gripper and the human arm remain visible, which is the residual gap the adapter must absorb. 

E.5. Other Robots 

Astribot S1 [1] and Spirit Moz1 [39] are wheeled dual-arm humanoids with a head camera and two wrist cameras. Their views are used as recorded. Their joint states are re-ordered into the shared layout (seven joints per arm, one opening per gripper, base x, y, ψ ), and the base is expressed relative to the body frame as for the executor. Arm kinematics, torso articulation and camera placement still differ, which is why the embodiment adapter (Sec. C) is trained together with these sources’ SRAs. 

## F. Experiments 

This section follows Sec. 4. We first define the real-world benchmark and the baselines (Secs. F.1 and F.2) and give the results of Tab. 2 per task (Sec. F.3). We then show how the predicted actions and the executed behavior depend on the demonstration (Sec. F.4), give the RoboTwin 2.0 protocol (Sec. F.5), and expand scaling and ablations per task, including two ablations of the full model (Sec. F.6). Finally, we turn to the source-routed adapters that admit other demonstrators (Sec. F.7) and to progress tracking and recovery (Sec. F.8). 

F.1. Real-World Benchmark 

Tasks. Table S3 describes the twelve tasks of Fig. 4. Task Generalization composes skills seen in pretraining into new 19 goals, step orders and object combinations; Skill Extension requires skills beyond pretraining, such as handling a cloth bag, an articulated cabinet or bimanual pouring. A task is long-horizon ( †) if its demonstration has at least five steps. 

Properties. The twelve tasks were defined after the pre-training corpus was frozen, and no recording of them, in any scene, is part of it. For each task we also retrieved the 20 corpus task groups with the most similar instruction (Qwen3-Embedding-8B) and inspected them by hand: none has the same goal and object combination. 

Trials. Each task is evaluated in 100 trials per method, split over five demonstrations and five test scenes. The demon-strations were recorded once, before any policy was run, in scenes different from all test scenes. Within a scene, object instances and placements are re-randomized from a fixed list for every trial, the same for all methods, and methods are evaluated in interleaved blocks of ten trials. An operator scores each trial with the criteria of Tab. S3, within a time limit of twice the demonstration duration. 

F.2. Baselines 

VLAs. π0.5 [32] and GR00T N1.7 [28] are fine-tuned from their public checkpoints on 50 teleoperated demonstrations of each evaluation task. With 20 demonstrations per task, the number given to the in-context policies on Skill Extension, they reach only 19.2% and 23.5% on average, so we report the 50-demonstration setting. 

In-context policies. HOST [3] and Zero-WAM [49] are retrained with their official architectures and recipes on our robot pairs; HOST’s learned aligner is trained on our DTW alignments, and Zero-WAM receives the full demonstration as a prefix. On Skill Extension they are adapted with the same 20 demonstrations per task as Any2Act. 

F.3. Real-World Results per Task 

Table S4 repeats Tab. 2 with 95% Wilson intervals for Any2Act. Over the 600 trials of each benchmark, Any2Act exceeds the strongest baseline, GR00T N1.7, by 10.0 points on Task Generalization and by 25.2 points on Skill Extension (p < 0.001 for both, one-sided Fisher exact test). The largest margins are on Gathering, Sorting and Pouring, where the demonstration conveys the order of the steps and the coordi-nation of the two arms. 

F.4. Sensitivity to the Demonstration 

An in-context policy should change its behavior when the demonstration shows something different, but not when an-other demonstration of the same task is given. We test this offline on the predicted actions and on the real robot. 

Action error. On the six Task Generalization tasks, which share one set of weights, we take 50 query chunks (2 s, 14 arm joints) per task from 10 executor recordings of the task, disjoint from its demonstrations, and predict every chunk with each of the five demonstrations of the task ( same task )and with the 25 demonstrations of the other five tasks ( other task ); the window is placed by the deployed tracker. Table S5 shows that the five demonstrations of a task lead to nearly the same error (32.0–35.7 ×10 −3 rad on average), whereas a demonstration of another task raises it by 20.1 ×10 −3 rad (59%), with every interval above zero. 

Correct vs . wrong demonstrations. On the real robot, we give the policy either a correct demonstration of the instructed task or a wrong one that conflicts with the instruc-tion, e.g . by showing another object or another step order. We use six tasks: two pick-and-place tasks, two long-horizon placing tasks whose step order is easily confused, book ex-traction and pouring, with 50 trials per task and condition. With the correct demonstration (Tab. S6), the robot keeps the instructed order in 96.3% of the trials and succeeds in 67.3%. With a wrong one, success falls to 23.7%, and on the two order-sensitive tasks only 14 and 32% of the trials keep the instructed order. Both offline and on the robot, the policy executes what the demonstration shows, not only what the instruction or the current scene suggests. 

F.5. RoboTwin 2.0 

We follow the cross-task protocol of Zero-WAM [49] on RoboTwin 2.0 [4]. Starting from the 10K-hour checkpoint, Any2Act is post-trained on demonstration →query pairs of the 43 seen tasks (50 demonstrations per task, 20K steps) and evaluated on the 7 unseen tasks with three seeds of 100 rollouts. Each rollout receives as its demonstration one simulated execution of the unseen task, recorded with a different seed and rendered from RoboTwin’s head and wrist cameras, with its joint states. No adapter is used, since simulation is the post-training domain. Baseline numbers are taken from [49].

## G. Discussion 

In-context learning and task data. On Task Generaliza-tion, Any2Act uses no task data: each task is performed from a single demonstration with the pretrained weights. Skill Extension asks for skills beyond pretraining; there every method, including the baselines, receives executor data of the task, and Any2Act receives less than the VLAs (20 vs . 50 demonstrations per task). The demonstration remains essen-tial after this adaptation: without it, a language-conditioned model fine-tuned on the same 20 demonstrations falls from 35.2% to 9.5% at 400 hours, and to 0% on Relaying and Storing (Tab. S7). 

Cost of a new source. A translation pipeline is built once per source and reused for every task. Its adapter is trained once, on 200 pairs from 20 tasks disjoint from the evaluation tasks, for 500 steps with the pretrained model frozen (Sec. 4.5). Because each adapter acts only on its own source’s demonstration tokens, adding a source leaves the other sources and the executor’s own demonstrations exactly unchanged (Sec. C), and unlike full fine-tuning it carries over to unseen tasks (Sec. F.7). 

Robustness of progress tracking. The tracker only needs to keep the correct phase of the demonstration inside the window; the policy, trained on perturbed and time-rescaled windows, locates its progress within it (Sec. B.2). The win-dow tolerates a lag of up to 3 s, and rollback returns it to an earlier phase after a failed step, so pauses, retries and speed differences between demonstrator and executor are handled without a learned aligner per source (Tab. 6).

Limitations and future work. Two directions remain open. First, the interface to an LLM agent is still narrow: the agent passes one demonstration of the intended task, whereas a more general interface would let it compose demonstrations of several subtasks, edit them, or generate them for tasks nobody has shown, as our simulation source begins to do. Second, the unified representation conveys what a demon-stration looks like and how the arms move, but not how hard the hands press. Contact-rich tasks, such as inserting, wiping or tightening, depend on forces and touch that neither views nor joint trajectories record. Admitting force and tactile demonstrations, and recovering them from human videos through full 3D modeling of hand–object interaction rather than the object-level contacts ego2sim keeps today, would let a demonstration specify not only the motion but also the contact. 

Broader impact. Following demonstrations from human videos makes it easier to instruct robots without program-ming, which can make assistive robotics more accessible. The same ease raises the question of whose demonstrations a robot should follow; deployment should restrict the accepted sources and keep a human able to stop the robot at any time. All human videos used in this work were recorded by con-senting participants, and no faces are stored in the released material. 23
