## Abstract 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models achieve high suc-cess rates on the LIBERO benchmark but remain sensitive to paraphrasing. Existing VLAs ranging from 0.6B to 7.5B parameters experience a 22–52 point performance drop on LIBERO-Para while SmolVLA exhibits an even larger de-cline, from 83% to 8%. Furthermore, because most VLAs jointly learn language understanding and action genera-tion, it is difficult to determine whether a failure origi-nates from misunderstanding an instruction or executing it incorrectly. To address these challenges, we introduce CoBoVLA, or CodeBookVLA, a 250M-parameter VLA that decouples language understanding, planning, and control through learned discrete interpretable codes. A text encoder maps the instructions to shared step codes, which a visual planner then converts into action code sequences executed by a language-independent actor. This design improves ro-bustness to instruction paraphrasing while allowing fail-ures to be traced to individual stages. It also enables ef-ficient real-time control on resource-constrained hardware by encoding instructions only once and using compact dis-crete representations to keep each component lightweight. CoBoVLA achieves 91.4% success on LIBERO-Goal, com-pared with 82.8% for SmolVLA, under a matched proto-col, with 17 ms per control step latency. On LIBERO-Para, it achieves 81.1% across 4,092 unseen paraphrases and 78.5% on matched episodes, compared with 36.5% for SmolVLA LoRA fine-tuned on paraphrases. Without two-step demonstrations, CoBoVLA also completes 48.8% of 160 free-form two-step tasks. Finally, success reaches 91% when instructions are correctly encoded but falls to 27.2% otherwise, identifying language understanding as the main source of remaining failures. Together, these results demon-strate that decoupling language understanding and control can improve instruction robustness while making VLA fail-ures easier to diagnose. 

## 1. Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models map camera images and a language instruction to robot actions [1, 10, 15, 33], and on the LIBERO benchmark [25] the strongest ones now reach 96–98% success [10, 16, 35, 40]. These scores say lit-tle about language understanding, since a 0.5M-parameter 1arXiv:2611.22395v1 [cs.CV] 17 Nov 2026 policy that uses a lookup table of 40 task identifiers in place of language gets 95.8% on LIBERO [31], while paraphras-ing the instructions reduces the success of VLAs with 0.6B– 7.5B parameters by 22–52 points [14]. We argue that part of this difficulty comes from the archi-tecture. In a VLA, language and control are learned jointly in one network, so the representation of the instruction is mixed with the representation of the motion. This leads to three problems. First, the robustness to rewording has to be learned from robot data, or it has to be added at test time. Second, when the robot fails, the model does not tell us whether it misunderstood the instruction or executed the motion wrongly (Fig. 1). Third, the whole network, including its language model, runs at every control step, which makes real-time control difficult on the resource-constrained hardware of a robot. To address these problems, we explore a different de-sign, where understanding and acting communicate only through a discrete interface which can be inspected. The proposed CoBoVLA ( Co de Bo ok VLA, Fig. 2) has three stages. A text encoder (stage 1) maps the instruction to a short sequence of discrete step codes, one for each step of the task, and it is trained so that paraphrases of the same instruction are mapped to the same codes (Sec. 3.2). Then, a visual planner takes the current step code together with camera images and robot state, and predicts a chain of action codes, i.e ., discrete motion segments which are learned from demonstrations with minimum-description-length (MDL) segmentation (Secs. 3.1 and 3.3). An actor which never sees the language converts the current action codes into motor commands (Sec. 3.4). With this design, paraphrases that are mapped to the same step codes produce the same behavior, because no part after stage 1 sees the words. Also, every failure can be localized to one stage by reading the codes, and the language stage can be improved with text only: all of the paraphrase robustness in this pa-per comes from training stage 1 on generated paraphrases, without collecting new robot demonstrations. Finally, the architecture is chosen to run in real time on such hardware. The instruction is encoded once, by a 38M-parameter text encoder, instead of by a language model at every control step, and each stage can be small because the discrete in-terface absorbs its errors: an imprecise output of a small model still selects the right code as long as it lands closer to that code than to any other. On an RTX 4050 laptop GPU, one call of the planner and actor takes 74 ms and is made every 5 control steps (17 ms per step), well within the 250 ms that these steps take at LIBERO’s 20 Hz control rate, while larger VLAs take 87–219 ms per call on a faster RTX 4090 [40] (Sec. 4.2). We evaluate CoBoVLA on LIBERO-Goal [25] and on all 4,092 paraphrases of LIBERO-Para [14], and compare it with SmolVLA [33] and with SmolVLA fine-tuned on the same paraphrases (Sec. 4.1). With only 250M parameters, CoBoVLA succeeds in 91.4% of 500 standard LIBERO-Goal episodes (SmolVLA: 82.8%, p < 10 −4, two-sided sign test on matched episodes; all p-values in this paper use this test). On LIBERO-Para, it succeeds in 81.1% of the episodes without seeing any of the paraphrases during train-ing, which is the highest success reported on this benchmark so far (under our protocol, Tab. 3). Moreover, the step codes allow CoBoVLA to follow one sentence which asks for two tasks in sequence. Without any two-step demonstration, CoBoVLA completes both steps in 48.8% of 160 episodes, while SmolVLA does not complete the first step in any of the 80 episodes (Tab. 1). Analyzing the codes also shows where the remaining er-rors are (Sec. 4.5). When stage 1 outputs the canonical step codes, the success is 91.0%, which is close to the success with standard instructions (91.4%), but when it does not, the success drops to 27.2% (Tab. 4). In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We propose CoBoVLA, a VLA which connects lan-guage understanding and control only through discrete step codes and learned action codes, designed to run in real time on a laptop GPU (Sec. 3). Replacing the step codes with their continuous pre-quantization vectors low-ers the success on both benchmarks (Sec. 4.6). • We provide a controlled evaluation on LIBERO-Goal and the full LIBERO-Para benchmark under one protocol, with a one-time confirmation on held-out initial states (Sec. 4). • We show the execution of free-form two-step instructions and attribute the failures to parsing, step switching and execution (Sec. 4.3). • We present a code-level failure analysis on LIBERO-Para which separates understanding, planning and execution errors, and places most of the remaining paraphrase gap in the language stage (Sec. 4.5). 

## 2. Related Work 

Vision-language-action models. Most VLAs fine-tune a pretrained vision-language model to output robot actions, as discrete tokens in OpenVLA [15] or through a continu-ous action head trained with diffusion [5] or flow match-ing [1, 10, 16, 47]. Several works make them smaller and faster: SmolVLA [33] and VLA-Adapter [35] build on backbones of about 0.5B parameters, NanoVLA [3] routes between a small and a large model, and TurboVLA [40] drops the language model from the control path and gets 97.7% on LIBERO with 0.2B parameters.

icy on rephrased instructions [19, 22, 32, 39]; on LIBERO-Spatial with their own paraphrases, Li et al . [22] remove nearly all of the paraphrase cost by LoRA fine-tuning of OpenVLA on paraphrases. RoVLA [26] adds consis-tency constraints across rephrasings. At test time, Watts and Cui [36] have an LLM rewrite the instruction, which takes seconds per instruction, and Kwok et al . [20] ver-ify candidate actions against rephrasings at the price of many policy calls per step. The work closest to ours is GSR/ParaVLA [42], which decouples task semantics from visual features and reaches 72.5% on LIBERO-Para with a 0.33B model trained on canonical instructions only. In all of these methods, the instruction is still an input to the pol-icy; in CoBoVLA only stage 1 reads it, so the controller is invariant to wording and each failure can be traced to a stage. 

Discrete skills and latent plans. LISA [8] maps language to discrete skill codes that condition a language-free policy and studies their interpretability, and LADS [11] trains a language-regularized discrete latent-plan space and a high-level policy over it in separate stages. SkillDiffuser [23] plans over interpretable skill abstractions. QueST [27] and STAR [21] learn discrete skill tokens on LIBERO, and sev-eral recent VLAs plan in a coarse discrete space before act-ing [4, 37]. Our action codes come from an MDL segmen-tation of the demonstrations into variable-length segments, following CompILE [17], LOVE [12] and LAST [7]. These build on the minimum-description-length principle [29]; PRISE [48] and Subwords as Skills [43] instead compress quantized action sequences with byte-pair encoding, and earlier work segmented demonstrations by changepoint de-tection [18]. What we add is the use of such an interface for robustness to wording in a VLA, with a language stage trained on paraphrases alone, and the use of the codes to attribute failures on a paraphrase benchmark. 

## 3. Method 

CoBoVLA maps an instruction ℓ and observations ot to ac-tions at through two discrete interfaces (Fig. 2), where t

indexes control steps. The observation ot consists of two camera images (front and wrist) and the robot state st ∈ R8

is the number of steps in the instruction and each zk is a tuple of M = 6 discrete codes (Sec. 3.2). For the current step k, the planner maps (zk, o t) to a chain of action codes 

ct:t+H : the codes of the motion segment that is executing at step t and of the segments that follow it, H = 3 segments in total (Sec. 3.3). The actor maps (ct:t+H , o t) to a chunk of actions and never receives ℓ or z (Sec. 3.4). We first de-scribe the action codes (Sec. 3.1). All visual inputs use the 3SigLIP [45] vision encoder of SmolVLA [33] at 256 × 256

3.1. Action codes from MDL segmentation 

We segment each demonstration into variable length mo-tion segments. This motion segment is represented using a two level code using a VQ-VAE style encoder [34] with residual vector quantization [44]. The code is represented as c = ( c(1) , c (2) ), where c(1) and c(2) are each chosen from its own learned codebook of K = 32 entries. The first level covers coarser motion covering plan level details while the next level covers more refined correction codes which level 1 missed. We follow minimum description length (MDL) principle to come up with the best segmentation that describes each demonstration in the fewest bits. Cutting too finely pays for many codes and cutting too coarsely pays in reconstruction error.

The codebook is learned once on all 1,693 LIBERO demonstrations [25] and then frozen. 

3.2. Stage 1: instructions to step codes 

We use bge-base [38], top two layers fine-tuned, to encode the sentence into learned slots ( M = 6 ). These slots are built by attending to the word tokens. All slots share a learned cosine codebook of Kz = 32 entries, and the slot values are replaced by their nearest codebook entries. The training signal comes from the paraphrase pairs (ℓA, ℓ B )

of the same instruction. The codes of ℓA must predict the codes of ℓB and support decoding ℓB . We also add com-mitment loss, a batch-entropy term against collapse, and a penalty on repeated slots. This is done to maintain the ro-bustness across paraphrasing to allow the codes to learn the meaning rather than attaching words to them. 

Paraphrase data. We generate 27,222 paraphrases with a large language model (Claude Sonnet 5.5) for the 40 LIBERO instructions and 191 recombined instructions built from the same objects and actions, and remove the 74 that exactly match a LIBERO-Para paraphrase. LIBERO-Para is used only for testing. 

Step coder. Instructions can describe more than one step. The deployed stage 1 is a smaller step coder (bge-small [38], 38.4M parameters, top four layers fine-tuned) with one query embedding per step and a presence head that predicts how many steps the instruction contains. It is trained to output, for every step, the meaning-codebook codes of that step’s canonical instruction, on single-step paraphrases, on two-step sentences formed on the fly by joining paraphrases of two steps, and on about 1,500 two-step rewrites written by a language model. Pairs that form one of the 16 two-step test pairs, in either order, are ex-cluded from all of them. 

3.3. Planner: step codes to action-code chains 

The planner is a 6-layer transformer (width 512, 26.1M pa-rameters). At each call it predicts the next 3 action-code segments ct:t+H as a sequence of discrete tokens, plus a done probability for the current step. It is an autoregressive decoder: it cross-attends to a memory of 128 frozen SigLIP tokens (each camera’s 16 × 16 patch grid average-pooled to 

8 × 8), one robot-state token and the embeddings of the six step codes, and greedily decodes two tokens per segment, one per level, followed by an end-of-chain token. We train it with cross-entropy on the code chains of the LIBERO-Goal demonstrations, using the step codes of a randomly drawn paraphrase of the task. In a closed loop, a planner trained with behavior cloning alone drifts out of the demonstration distribution, so we run one round of DAgger [30]. We roll out the policy from ini-tial states 10–29 (disjoint from the evaluation states 0–9), label each visited state with the code of the nearest training frame of the same task in terms of SigLIP similarity and state distance, and retrain on the demonstrations together with the relabeled states. This nearest-frame lookup is only used to make training labels; at test time all codes come from the planner. The done probability is the probability that the chain starts with an end-of-chain token. We fine-tune it with binary cross-entropy on 80 two-step rollouts recorded with oracle step switching and labeled by whether the goal of the current step holds; these use the 16 two-step test pairs, but initial states 10–14 rather than the test initial states 0–9. 43.4. Actor: action codes to motor commands 

The actor is a flow-matching transformer [24] (12 layers, width 512) that predicts a chunk of future actions from the SigLIP image tokens, the robot state and the current action codes. It gets no language input. Each of its blocks applies self-attention over one sequence of 128 pooled SigLIP to-kens, a state token, six code-slot tokens (the two-level codes of the three planned segments) and 50 noisy action tokens, and the flow time and the code slots modulate every block through adaLN-zero. Before entering this sequence, each code-slot token passes through two such blocks and then cross-attends to the full 16 × 16 patch grid of both cameras, so that the codes decide where the actor looks at full res-olution. We fine-tune the last four SigLIP layers at 0.2×

the learning rate, which together with the actor gives 99.4M trainable parameters. An auxiliary loss supervises the at-tention from the code slots to the image tokens with the fu-ture path of the gripper in the image plane. The actor is trained on LIBERO-Goal demonstrations with ground-truth codes and, with probability 0.5, with the codes the planner predicts for the same frame, so that it learns to cope with planner errors. At test time, planner and actor are called every 5 control steps, and the predicted actions are executed with tempo-ral ensembling [46]: an arm action is the 0.9age -weighted average of the predictions for that step from all chunks is-sued in the last 20 steps, where age is the number of control steps since the chunk was issued, and the gripper command comes from the newest chunk. 

3.5. Multi-step instructions 

For an instruction with S > 1 steps, the policy starts with 

z1. When the planner’s chain starts with the done token in three consecutive calls, a short scripted motion moves the arm back to its starting pose (median 57 control steps), the ensembling buffer is cleared and the planner continues with z2, as in SeqVLA [41]. No component is trained on two-step demonstrations; the sequence itself comes from stage 1. 

## 4. Experiments 

We perform four experiments. (1) We compare CoBoVLA’s performance against SmolVLA, a small VLA with 0.6B parameters, on both standard and paraphrased in-structions under a single protocol (Sec. 4.2). (2) Evaluate CoBoVLA on free-form two-step instructions without two-step demonstrations (Sec. 4.3). (3) Compare CoBoVLA against existing literature on the LIBERO-Para (Sec. 4.4). (4) Study what the learned codes reveal about the failures (Sec. 4.5). The concluding sub-section, Sec. 4.6, analyzes the design, and concludes why codes need to be discrete. 

4.1. Setup 

On LIBERO-Goal [25], we run all 500 episodes, spread across 10 tasks with 50 different initial states in shared kitchen scenes. Some baselines and ablations are run only on Goal-100, the first 10 initial states (0–9) of each LIBERO-Goal task (100 episodes); ParaVLA [42] uses the same 100 episodes for its LIBERO-Goal results and for its diagnostic and ablation experiments. LIBERO-Para [14] contains 4,092 paraphrases of the 10 LIBERO-Goal instruc-tions, grouped by what they change: the action expres-sion (870), the object reference (259), or both (compound, 2,963), and we run each paraphrase once. Paraphrase i of a task starts from initial state i mod 50 . For the most expen-sive baseline runs, we used a fixed 200-episode subset with about 20 paraphrases per task. For the two-step instructions, we built 16 tasks from ordered pairs of LIBERO-Goal tasks whose goals could be held together; success was defined as both goals held at the end of the episode. For even ini-tial states, a sentence was written by a language model that fused two steps, for example: “Pull the cabinet’s middle drawer out and then move the wine bottle onto the rack”, while for the odd ones, two LIBERO-Para paraphrases were joined, for example: “Position the serving bowl on the stove after you open the middle compartment of the cupboard”. For each of the tasks, we ran from initial states 0–9, giving a total of 160 episodes. 

Evaluation protocol. All results in Tabs. 1, 2 and 4 come from one simulator setup (MuJoCo with EGL rendering, 

256 × 256 images, episodes of at most 520 steps for single-step and 980 for two-step tasks). We found that the instruc-tion given to the policy matters: some LIBERO forks read it from the language field of the task file, which rewords 5 of the 10 LIBERO-Goal tasks ( e.g . “open the middle layer of the drawer”). On these five tasks (initial states 0–9), SmolVLA succeeds in 11/50 episodes with the reworded field and 37/50 with the task-file names, and CoBoVLA in 28/50 and 46/50. We use the task-file names, as in stan-dard LIBERO and in the training data. Under this protocol, the public SmolVLA LIBERO checkpoint reaches 82.8% on LIBERO-Goal, consistent with an independent rerun that gives 84.7 ±3.7% [28]. We defined two baselines, SmolVLA and SmolVLA + paraphrase LoRA. For both, we used the 605M-parameter released checkpoint of the SmolVLA [33] model. To sep-arately study the architecture from the paraphrase data, in SmolVLA + paraphrase LoRA, we fine-tune the SmolVLA with LoRA rank 32 on the language model and action expert (27.2M trainable parameters) for 3,000 steps, on LIBERO-Goal demonstrations whose instruction is replaced with a probability of 0.8 by a paraphrase from the same 27,222 paraphrases that train our stage 1. 4.2. Comparison under one protocol 

We compare our CoBoVLA with SmolVLA and its para-phrase LoRA variant. On the standard instructions, CoBoVLA achieves a 91.4% success rate across 500 episodes of LIBERO-Goal, whereas SmolVLA achieves 82.8%. The gap holds on the 300 episodes that excluded DAgger initial states (90.3% vs. 84.0%). Particularly, SmolVLA is better than CoBoVLA on two tasks, pushing the plate (45 vs. 40 out of 50) and the wine rack (42 vs. 39), and CoBoVLA is better or equal on the remaining eight. On the paraphrases, a much larger difference was ob-served. SmolVLA succeeds in 8% of the subset episodes, consistent with the reported 4.5% on the full benchmark un-der another protocol [42]. LoRA fine-tuning of SmolVLA on our paraphrases pushes performance to 36.5% but costs 16 points on the standard instructions (83 → 67 on Goal-100). CoBoVLA achieved 78.5% on the same 200 episodes and 81.1% on all 4,092 while maintaining the success rate on the standard instructions. The paraphrase drop is a modest 10.3 points. On the two-step instructions, SmolVLA failed on all of the 80 episodes in the first step, while CoBoVLA completed both steps in 48.8% of the 160 episodes, as reported in Sec. 4.3. CoBoVLA has a parameter count of 40% of SmolVLA (0.25B vs. 0.6B) and is 3.4 × times faster per inference call on the NVIDIA RTX 4050 laptop GPU (74 ms vs. 254 ms). Because CoBoVLA executes five actions per call, its cost per control step is just about 17 ms. LIBERO runs at 20 Hz, so a call every five control steps has 250 ms available, and CoBoVLA runs in real time on this GPU. On an RTX 4090, a considerably faster GPU than ours, TurboVLA [40] mea-sures one policy call (from input to action chunk, batch size 1) at 219.2 ms for π0, 212.7 ms for SmolVLA, 112.2 ms for OpenVLA-OFT, 87.3 ms for VLA-Adapter and 31.2 ms for TurboVLA; a CoBoVLA call takes 74 ms on the RTX 4050 laptop GPU. Peak GPU memory of CoBoVLA with a batch size of 1 is about 1.0 GB compared to 1.2 GB for SmolVLA. As for training, the entire CoBoVLA pipeline trains in about 8.5 h on the same RTX 4050 laptop GPU.

4.5. What the codes reveal 

Since both the interfaces use discrete codes, we can also trace failures to individual stages (Tab. 4). Codes develop structure. Although stage 1 is trained only to agree across the paraphrases, an emergent meaning-ful structure is observed in the six codes. Five slots repre-sent the goal, while the fourth slot represents the object. For example, the two tasks involving placing objects “on top of the cabinet” share goal code 16 but have different object codes (27 for the bowl and 17 for the wine bottle). 

The action codes are readable. Although the action codebook is learned without any labels, its first-level codes correspond to recognizable robot motion primitives. For ex-ample, Code 27 closes the gripper in 91% of the segments, while codes 30 and 10 open it in all the segments. Codes 25 and 7 move the open gripper forward and downward, while codes 0 and 18 lift and carry the objects with the gripper closed. Pick-and-place demonstrations typically begin with the same approach and descent sequence (codes 25 and 7, sometimes through 5), but differ in how the object is car-ried and placed. As a result, the planner’s predicted code sequence can be read as a sequence of actions: approach, descent, grasp, lift, carry, and place. 

Where to focus. Most of the failures on paraphrases are understanding errors, making stage 1 the clearest target for improvement. Improving this stage requires only the text data. Training the meaning codebook takes about an hour, and training the step coder takes about 5 minutes on an NVIDIA RTX 4050 laptop GPU. Either component can be updated independently without retraining the planner or the actor or collecting any additional robot data. 

4.6. Design analyses 

The development ablation in the supplementary material an-alyzes the contributions of different components in the final actor. Temporal ensembling is the only individually statis-tically significant change ( p = 0 .04 ). Individually, fine-tuning the vision tail and the path-supervised attention each improves the success rate by one or two percentage points, although these gains are within statistical noise. Combining them both gives the highest overall gain. 

Discrete versus continuous step codes. We train a con-tinuous planner using the same procedure and the data as the discrete planner. Instead of receiving discrete step codes, the planner receives the meaning codebook’s slot vectors before quantization. On the LIBERO-Para subset, the dis-crete planner achieved 77% compared to 65.5% for the con-tinuous planner ( p = 0 .002 ). On Goal-100, the discrete planner succeeded in 93 while the continuous planner suc-ceeded in 82 ( p = 0 .03 ). These results suggest that the benefit of discrete codes goes beyond mapping different wordings of the same instruction to a common representa-tion, consistent with the codes absorbing the imprecision of the small text model (Sec. 1). However, each planner was trained with only one random seed. 

## 5. Limitations 

Following ParaVLA [42], we trained and evaluated CoBoVLA only on LIBERO-Goal, as LIBERO-Para is con-structed from this suite. Furthermore, all the experiments were conducted in simulation. One limitation of LIBERO-Para is that it contains para-phrases of only 10 underlying instructions. A simpler ap-proach could be to identify the closest matching instruction from the original set and pass it to an existing strong policy. We haven’t evaluated this alternative. First, the multi-step execution relies on a scripted return to the starting pose between the steps. Additionally, the sec-ond step can sometimes interfere with the outcomes of the first (Sec. 4.3). Second, disabling the path-supervised atten-tion at test time doesn’t change the actor’s validation loss, making the hypotheses hard to validate. Finally, some ini-tial states used during DAgger training overlap with those in the standard evaluation set. We also report the results on the 300 evaluation episodes that exclude these overlapping states (Sec. 4.2). 

## 6. Conclusion 

We introduced CoBoVLA, a framework that connects language understanding, planning, and robotic con-trol through discrete, learnable codes. Its architecture is chosen to run in real time on resource-constrained hardware: the instruction is encoded once by a small text encoder, and because the discrete codes absorb the errors of small models, every stage stays small, so that CoBoVLA needs 74 ms per inference call and 1.0 GB of GPU memory on a laptop GPU. Despite having only 250M params, CoBoVLA outperforms SmolVLA on the standard LIBERO-Goal instructions under the same proto-col and achieves the highest reported success rate on the LIBERO-Para benchmark. The framework also supports free-form two-step instructions that SmolVLA cannot directly execute. Our analyses show that most remaining failures on the paraphrased instructions originate from incorrect language understanding, which can be improved with the text data alone. These findings highlight the value of decoupling language understanding from robotic control and suggest that VLA systems should be evaluated not only on their task success rate, but also by how effectively 8their failures can be understood, diagnosed, and addressed.

## A. Implementation details 

Model size. The deployed model has 250.3M parame-ters: stage 1 (38.4M), the shared frozen SigLIP encoder (86.4M), the planner (26.1M) and the actor (99.4M). The actor’s count includes its own fine-tuned copy of the last four SigLIP layers (28.4M); since the planner still reads the frozen layers, both copies are deployed and both are counted. Table S1 lists the hyperparameters of every trained com-ponent; all are taken from the arguments stored with the de-ployed checkpoints. All components use AdamW. LIBERO task indices 10–19 are the ten LIBERO-Goal tasks; the planner and the actor are trained on their 428 demon-strations only, the action codebook on all 1,693 LIBERO demonstrations. 

Action codes. A segment’s 7-D actions (6 end-effector deltas and the gripper command, normalized per dimen-sion) are resampled to 16 steps and concatenated with the segment’s log-duration; an MLP encoder maps them to a 64-D vector that two residual EMA vector quantizers (32 entries each, dead entries restarted) encode, and an MLP de-coder reconstructs the 16 steps, which are resampled back to the segment’s true length so that the cost in Eq. (1) is additive in original time. The reconstruction term uses a Gaussian whose per-dimension variance is re-estimated ev-ery round; the code cost is log 32 nats for the second level and log(192 − 6 + 1) for the duration (segments are 6 to 192 steps long), and the first-level bigram prior is estimated per task with Laplace smoothing on the training demonstra-tions. Each of the 8 rounds re-segments every demonstra-tion by dynamic programming and then trains the autoen-coder for 3,000 steps on the new segments. 10% of the demonstrations of every task are held out from autoencoder training to measure reconstruction (Sec. E); all 1,693 are segmented. 

Meaning codebook. It is trained on paraphrase pairs of 231 instructions (40 LIBERO and 191 recombined, Sec. B); 28 of the recombined instructions are held out entirely to test generalization to unseen meanings, and 10% of the paraphrases of the others are held out for validation, leaving 21,880 training sentences. Each step samples two different sentences of one instruction, ℓA and ℓB . A two-layer de-coder generates ℓB ’s tokens from ℓA’s six quantized codes; a swap loss makes ℓA’s slot logits predict ℓB ’s codes and vice versa, enabled after 1,000 decode-only steps so that the codes first carry meaning; a commitment loss, a per-slot batch-entropy bonus against codebook collapse and a penalty on the overlap of the code distributions of different slots complete the objective. The lower layers of bge-base are frozen and their outputs cached; the top two are fine-tuned. After training, the 10 LIBERO-Goal instructions re-ceive 10 distinct code tuples (Tab. S11). 

Step coder. The step coder replaces bge-base with bge-small (top four layers fine-tuned), keeps the meaning code-book frozen, and gives the six slot queries a learned per-step embedding initialized at zero, so that at initialization every step reproduces the single-step codes. It predicts up to three steps and a presence probability per step; the number of steps is the index of the first absent step. Atraining batch mixes single-step paraphrases (35%), LLM-fused two-step rewrites of training pairs (20%) and two-step sentences joined on the fly from paraphrases of two steps (45%); half of the joined sentences pair two LIBERO-Goal tasks. The targets are the meaning-codebook codes of each step’s canonical instruction, with a cross-entropy loss per slot and a binary cross-entropy loss on presence. 

Planner. The planner’s memory holds the SigLIP tokens of both cameras ( 256 × 256 input, average-pooled to 8 × 8

tokens per camera), the robot state and the six step codes; a 6-layer decoder generates the action-code chain greedily, two tokens (levels 1 and 2) per segment for three segments, followed by an end-of-chain token. In training, each frame’s step codes are those of a random paraphrase of its task, and each code is replaced by a random one with probability 0.05; images are randomly shifted by up to 8 pixels. The behavior-cloned planner is trained for 3,000 steps with half of every batch drawn from the DAgger states. They come from 200 rollouts of an earlier behavior-cloned planner (20 per task): from initial states 10–19 the planner’s codes are always executed, and from initial states 20–29 each call exe-cutes the labeling oracle’s codes with probability 0.5. Every state at which the planner was called is recorded and labeled with the code of the nearest training frame of the same task, giving 10,601 states. The done fine-tuning then trains for 1,000 more steps, adding to every batch 16 states from the 80 two-step rollouts of Sec. 3.3 (9,795 states, 1,299 of them labeled done).

The code atten-tion is a set of glimpses: after two context blocks over the image tokens, the state and the codes, each code slot forms one query per camera over the full 16 × 16 patch grid, and what it reads is added to that slot’s token. The auxiliary loss is a cross-entropy between the front camera’s attention map of a slot and a target map built from the gripper’s projected image path during the segment that the slot covers: a sum of Gaussians ( σ = 12 pixels) over the path, with half of the mass on the segment’s end point, weighted by 0.1. The at-tention follows the path (on held-out frames, 80% of a slot’s attention falls on the 10 patches that the target ranks highest, against 4% for uniform attention), but the actor barely uses what it reads: setting the glimpses to zero changes the held-out flow loss of the deployed actor from 0.493 to 0.488. This agrees with the development ablation (Tab. S5), where adding the path-supervised attention changes success by one episode out of 200. Flow matching follows SmolVLA:

## B. Data 

Paraphrases. Training paraphrases were generated with Claude Sonnet through its command-line interface, from the instruction alone (no images). First, the model wrote new instructions that recombine the actions, objects and places of the 40 LIBERO training instructions; after removing ex-act repeats this gave 191 recombined instructions, for which no demonstrations exist and which only serve to teach the meaning codebook to factor meaning. Then, for each of the 231 instructions, two calls with different emphases asked 13 for 40 paraphrases each, and one more round asked for para-phrases that refer to every object and place by a description. The paraphrase prompt was:      

> You write training data for a robot that follows spoken instruc-tions in a tabletop kitchen scene. Rewrite the instruction be-low in exactly ndifferent ways that a person might say it. Ev-ery rewrite must ask for exactly the same action on exactly the same objects and locations; do not add, drop or change any object, location, count or step. [emphasis] Keep each one a single sentence or two short ones. Output ONLY a JSON array of nstrings, nothing else.

The two emphases asked for (i) varied everyday wording (synonyms, reordered clauses, polite requests, questions, indirect requests, casual speech) and (ii) less common word-ing (descriptions of objects and places by colour, shape, material, use or position, uncommon verbs, extra harmless context, hints and statements of need). After deduplica-tion there are 27,222 paraphrases, 4,652 of them for the 40 LIBERO instructions and 105–117 for each LIBERO-Goal instruction. The 74 that exactly matched a LIBERO-Para paraphrase (after lowercasing and removing punctu-ation) were removed; LIBERO-Para was never shown to the generator. Sample paraphrases of “put the bowl on top of the cabinet”: “Bowl goes on top of the cabinet.” , “Set the curved food container on the upper surface of the cup-board.” , “Place the round dish you’d eat soup or cereal from onto the upper surface of the storage unit.” 

Two-step sentences. The 16 test pairs are ordered pairs of LIBERO-Goal tasks whose two goals can hold at the same time in the shared scene (Tab. S6). Every unordered pair of steps that forms a test pair is excluded from all stage-1 training data, in either order. Training uses two kinds of two-step sentences built from the other pairs. Joined sen-tences connect two paraphrases with one of 19 templates, three of which name the second step first ( e.g . “before you 

B, A”, “ B after you A”); 8,986 are precomputed, and more are formed on the fly. Fused sentences (1,528) are single-sentence rewrites written by Claude Haiku, which was asked for both steps in one or two sentences with the order unambiguous, varied wording, pronouns where natu-ral, and occasionally the second step mentioned first. The test sentences are built the same way from the 16 held-out pairs: for even initial states a fused sentence (482 generated, 

e.g . “Pull the cabinet’s middle drawer out and then move the wine bottle onto the rack”), and for odd initial states two LIBERO-Para paraphrases of the two steps joined by a template (640 generated, e.g . “Position the serving bowl on the stove after you open the middle compartment of the cupboard”). 

## C. Per-task results 

Tables S2 and S3 give the per-task results behind Sec. 4.2, Sec. 4.5 and the discrete-versus-continuous comparison; Tab. S10 splits the full LIBERO-Para results by the bench-mark’s paraphrase types. Three tasks account for most understanding errors: “open the top drawer and put the bowl inside” (110), and the two “on top of the cabinet” tasks (64 and 112), whose paraphrases often call the cabinet a sideboard , hutch or cup-board (Tab. S7). When stage 1 gets the codes right, success is at least 87.8% on every task except two of the three weak-est tasks on standard instructions (Tab. S2), “put the cream cheese in the bowl” (75.1%) and “put the wine bottle on the rack” (80.5%). Paraphrases that only change how the action is ex-pressed are almost always understood (87.8–100% canon-ical codes); the questions and hints are the hardest of them (77.0% and 81.8% success). Changing the name of an ob-ject by a contextual synonym ( e.g . vessel for bowl, side-board for cabinet) is the most damaging change, alone (78.2%) or combined with any change of the action ex-pression (68.8–73.3%), and in the compound rows success is within 7 points of the canonical-code rate, which again places the remaining gap in stage 1. 

## D. Two-step instructions per pair
