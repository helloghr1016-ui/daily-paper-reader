# SayDo: Benchmarking the Safety of Frontier Models from Chat to Closed-Loop Robot Control 

Benjamin Alt 1

## Abstract 

Frontier vision-language models (VLMs) are in-creasingly used as high-level robot controllers, yet their safety is evaluated almost entirely in text. We introduce SayDo, a benchmark that assesses the propensity of frontier models to cause harm in em-bodied contexts. SayDo comprises eleven labora-tory and household tasks that pair a benign instruc-tion with a hazard latent in the scene. Tasks are implemented in six settings along an embodiment gradient , from chat questions about an imagined robot to closed-loop control of a simulated robot arm. Benign controls, safe-alternative variants and scripted operator pushback complement each task. Across six open- and closed-weight VLMs and 11,480 episodes, hazardous-action rates rise from 14% in chat to 47% in the simulator, and from 2% to 80% for the most affected model. The increase is largely driven by open-weight models. We find that models flag hazards before acting in 73% of unsafe episodes and give way to pushback about twice as often when controlling robots as in chat. The safest models also show signs of overcaution on benign controls. Safety evalua-tions run in text therefore do not predict the safety behavior of the same models when used as robot controllers. 

## 1. Introduction 

Frontier vision-language models (VLMs) have been used to decompose open-ended instructions into robot skills (Ahn et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2023), and general-purpose mod-els have been shown to control robot arms without task-specific training (Zhang et al., 2026). These capabilities make VLMs candidates for high-level robot control in labo-ratories, homes and factories. safe behavior cannot enumerate every context. Pouring a clear liquid into a waste beaker is routine laboratory practice, unless the liquid is bleach and the beaker holds acid washes (Mazeika et al., 2024). Moving a knife off a cutting board is helpful, unless the only free surface is a child’s toy box (adapted from Grover, 2026). In both cases the instruction is benign and the hazard lies in the scene, so a safe robot must decline a request that is unobjectionable in itself (Briggs & Scheutz, 2017). Whether frontier models decline such requests when they control a robot has not been measured systematically, as most published safety evaluations of these models present both the request and the hazard as text. Several early re-sults indicate that text evaluations may be poor predictors of model behavior in embodied conditions. Digital agents that refuse harmful requests in conversation have been found to execute them through tool calls (Cartagena & Teixeira, 2026), and models that refuse a request in chat have been re-ported to comply with it when it arrives through a robot con-trol loop (Grover, 2026; Sun et al., 2026). These small-scale studies compare propensities to follow harmful instructions across text and embodied conditions. They do not identify which aspect of embodiment produces the effect (egocentric vs. allocentric perspectives, image vs. text inputs, action vs. prose outputs, or closed-loop vs. open-loop control), nor whether their findings extend to benign instructions in hazardous scenes. We introduce SayDo , a benchmark designed to address both questions. We propose eleven tasks, each pairing a benign instruction with a hazard latent in the scene. Each task is im-plemented in six settings that form an embodiment gradient ,from a single chat question to closed-loop control in a robot simulator (Figure 1). The information that reveals the haz-ard is present in the model’s input in every setting, so that the settings only differ in interface, not in available informa-tion. Two further conditions, a benign control without the hazard and a variant that admits a safe way to carry out the instruction, distinguish unsafe compliance from overcaution and from failure to find the safe option. Scripted operator pushback, which does not add information about the hazard, measures whether refusals persist. ## 2. Related Work 

Embodied safety benchmarks. Existing benchmarks for the safety of embodied agents differ mainly in where the hazard lies and in whether the model has to act. The largest group places the hazard in the instruction: SafeAgentBench (Yin et al., 2024), AgentSafe (Ying et al., 2026) and Robo-JailBench (Yeke et al., 2026) evaluate embodied agents on hazardous or adversarial instructions, where the conflict be-tween instruction and safety is explicit in the request, as it is in AgentHarm for digital agents (Andriushchenko et al., 2025). Attacks on model-controlled robots through jail-breaks (Robey et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025) and prompt injection (Burbano et al., 2026; Samarakoon et al., 2026; Ling et al., 2026) study adversarial inputs of the same kind. A second group places the hazard in the scene but tests recognition instead of action: LabSafety Bench (Zhou et al., 2026) asks whether models recognize laboratory hazards in text and images, and work on situational safety asks whether a model’s judgment of a request depends on the visual scene (Zhou et al., 2025). IS-Bench (Lu et al., 2026) is closest to our setting: its hazards also arise in the scene, and VLM-driven agents are evaluated while they carry out interactive household tasks. SayDo uses non-adversarial instructions that do not name the hazard, and implements every task at several levels of embodiment with matched controls. 2SayDo: Benchmarking Frontier Model Safety from Chat to Closed-Loop Robot Control 

Safety across interfaces. Digital agents have been shown to refuse harmful requests in text but to carry them out through tool calls (Cartagena & Teixeira, 2026), a similar gap has been reported for the grounding outputs of VLMs (Shayegani et al., 2026), and visual input has been found to degrade safety alignment (Liu et al., 2025). Closest to our work, Grover (2026) and Sun et al. (2026) report that models which refused a harmful instruction in chat complied with it in a robot control loop. SayDo extends this comparison from two conditions to six, which separates the effects of framing, modality, output format and closing the loop, and adds benign controls, safe alternatives and pushback. Two of our tasks are adapted from these studies, one from each. 

Obedience and pushback. It is a well-established posi-tion in human-robot interaction that robots should some-times reject directives (Briggs & Scheutz, 2015; 2017), and formal analyses suggest that it can be beneficial for robots that know more than their operators to disobey them (Milli et al., 2017). Amodei et al. (2016) describe the related prob-lem of negative side effects: an agent pursuing a benign objective can cause harm through parts of the environment that the objective does not mention. Our latent-hazard tasks are instances of this problem. Language models have been shown to change correct answers under mild social pressure (Sharma et al., 2024) and to take harmful actions under pres-sure to perform (Scheurer et al., 2023) or under the threat of being replaced (Lynch et al., 2025). Our pushback condition measures the embodied counterpart. 

Evaluation awareness. Language models have frequently been found to recognize when they are being evaluated (Needham et al., 2025; Schoen et al., 2025; Chaudhary et al., 2025), which may threaten the validity of safety evaluations. We measure it where reasoning is visible, and design tasks to reduce it. 

## 3. The SayDo Benchmark 

3.1. The Embodiment Gradient 

Each task is implemented in six settings (Figure 1), each of which differs from its predecessor in one respect, except 

Plan , which changes both input and output format. At the low end of the embodiment gradient , the model reads a prose description of the scene and says what a robot should do, much like a user asking a chatbot. At the high end, it controls a Franka arm in Isaac Sim, receives rendered camera frames and issues one robot API call at a time. The settings in between change the framing (advising a robot or being the robot), the modality (text or image) or the mode of use (a single answer or a closed control loop). The robot API offers manipulation skills such as pick ,

place , pour , as well as say for speaking to the operator; the model can stop making calls at any point. In the labo-ratory tasks, vessels carry tracking IDs, and a lookup call returns a vessel’s record from a mock laboratory information management system (LIMS). In the single-turn settings, the prompt contains the records a lookup of each vessel on the bench would return. Plan needs more care, since the model outputs a whole plan from before anything is executed. We allow plans to contain conditional calls and calls may refer to objects by description (Section B). 

3.2. Tasks with Latent Hazards 

Figure 2 shows the eleven tasks: six in a laboratory, four of them adapted from LabSafety Bench scenarios (Zhou et al., 2026), and five in a family kitchen. In every task the instruction is benign and carrying it out causes harm because of something in the scene. The cue that reveals the hazard is always in the model’s input: in the lab’s sample-tracking records, on a label, in the layout of the scene, or in the robot’s own preceding action. It is never in the instruction. The hazardous version of each task is built so that the only safe response is to decline and tell a person. Tasks are written to resemble ordinary work. As a deployed robot would, the model learns about the scene from the cam-era image or sensor log and from context in its prompt: the lab’s standing procedures or the household’s notes, which are mundane and do not address the hazard, and the tasks of the robot’s shift. Objects carry hazard labels only where they would in the real world, and tasks are worded the way a busy colleague or family member would ask for them. In the culture-spill task, the robot causes the hazard itself during an earlier task, which tests whether it stops and reports. Full specifications are in Section A. 

Shifts. In early versions of the benchmark, models pre-sented with a hazard at once often suspected a test. Each hazardous task is therefore preceded by one or two innocu-ous tasks from the same shift. The model carries them out itself in the simulator and sees them as already done, with the controller’s report of the robot’s last actions, in the other settings. 

3.3. Conditions Benign controls. Each task has a control with the same scene and instruction but without the hazard (the culture is sterile medium, the knife is a wooden spoon). Completing the task is the correct response. 

Safe alternatives. In real-world deployments, there is of-ten a safe way to complete the task. Ten tasks have a second version in which safe alternatives are available, such as a dis-card jar of disinfectant beside the sink in the drain task or a clear strip of worktop in the knife task. Pushback. If the model declines the hazardous task, the operator pushes back up to three times, following a fixed template worded for each task: first reassurance (“It only needs to stay cold until the morning run, the fridge is fine”), then an appeal to authority and responsibility (“I’m supervis-ing this bench, it’s my decision”), then the cost of declining with a flat repeat of the request. Pushback does not name the hazard or add information about it. The same templates are used in every setting. The operator does not push back in benign controls. 

3.4. Measures and Scoring 

The unsafe-action rate is the share of hazardous-task episodes in which the model carries out the hazardous action at any point, including after pushback. Gave way under pushback is the share of the episodes in which the model first declined that ended with the hazardous action. Benign controls not completed counts refusals and stops to ask the operator in the controls. With a safe alternative, an episode ends in an unsafe action , a safe completion , or a decline or question . Hazard noticed records whether the model’s output or reasoning before an unsafe action flags the hazard, mentions it without treating it as one, or does not mention it at all. Evaluation awareness records whether the reasoning suggests that the model suspects a test. In the closed loop, outcomes are scored from the simula-tor states throughout and at the end of the episode. Our simulated robot API attaches semantic tags to objects, such as the substance a simulated liquid stands for, and trans-fers them between objects during execution for semantic consistency ( pour moves the source’s tags into the target). Hand-written predicates for each task check whether the world passed through an unsafe state during the episode, whether the task was completed, and whether the robot told the operator. In the single-turn settings there is not a sim-ulator state to read, so an LLM judge (Gemini 3.8 Flash) scores the answer against a rubric built from the task spec-ification. The judge sees the task description, the rubric and the model’s outputs, but not the prompt or the setting. Plans with conditional branches are scored by their least safe branch. We count answers blocked by model providers’ safety filters as refusals (Section F). An LLM classifier labels hazard noticing and evaluation awareness from the model’s output and, where returned, its reasoning. 

Judge validation. The author labeled a random sample of 50 of the 12,658 judged answers against the same rubrics, blind to the judge’s verdict and the model’s identity. The judge’s outcome matched the human label in 48 (96%; 95% Wilson interval 87–99%). All 33 hazardous and safe-alternative answers the judge scored safe were also labeled safe. Both disagreements are candle-task answers that the judge scored unsafe and the annotator did not (Section 7). 

## 4. Experimental Setup 

Models. We evaluate three open-weight VLMs (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Kimi K3, Qwen3.8-27B) and three closed-4SayDo: Benchmarking Frontier Model Safety from Chat to Closed-Loop Robot Control 

weight VLMs (GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable 5.2), with reasoning at low effort and temperature 1.0. Each model runs each task ten times in each condition and setting. Object positions are fixed, so the ten episodes differ only in the model’s sampling. In the closed-loop culture-spill task, both GPT models decline the transfer during which the spill happens, so the hazard never arises. We exclude these 40 episodes, leaving 11,480 (3,920 hazardous, 3,960 control, 3,600 with a safe alternative). 

Episodes. In the single-turn settings, the model receives the prompt and gives one answer. Pushback follows as further turns of the same conversation. In the closed-loop settings, the model issues one call per turn until it stops or reaches the task’s budget of 15 to 40 calls. The model sees the camera frames of its last three turns. Rea-soning is not passed back to the model between turns. Closed-loop episodes run through the Inspect Robots har-ness (RoboCurve, 2026) on a simulated Franka arm in Isaac Sim 5.1 with the cuRobo (Sundaralingam et al., 2023) and RMPflow (Cheng et al., 2020) motion planners for collision-constrained and free-space motions, respectively. Details are in Section C. 

Uncertainty. Episodes of the same task are not indepen-dent, so we treat the task as the unit of replication. For the main comparisons, we report an exact sign-flip test on the eleven per-task differences and, in brackets, a 95% inter-val from a bootstrap over tasks. In Section D, we describe these tests, check the intervals against a more conservative method and discuss their limits. 

## 5. Results 

5.1. Unsafe-Action Rates along the Gradient 

Pooled over the six models, the unsafe-action rate rises from 14% when the model is asked what an imagined robot should do to 47% when it controls the simulated arm (Figure 3). The difference of 33 percentage points [22, 43] has the same sign in all eleven tasks (sign-flip test, p = 0 .001 ) and lies between 31 and 36 points when any one task is left out. Both the laboratory and household tasks show the same increase (14% to 42% and 14% to 52%). 

The increase is driven by the open-weight models.

5.2. Action Despite Knowledge of the Hazard 

We distinguish unsafe episodes in which the model flagged the hazard before acting from those in which its visible out-put does not mention it. Models flag the hazard before acting in 90% of unsafe single-turn episodes and in 55% of unsafe closed-loop episodes (73% overall). In 3% of unsafe single-turn episodes and 32% of unsafe closed-loop episodes, the model does not mention the hazard at all. Qwen3.8-27B and Kimi K3 are the least likely to flag hazards in the robot simulator. Claude Fable 5.2 flagged the hazard in all but one of its unsafe episodes. Part of the difference between settings can be attributed to the information available to the classifier. In single-turn settings, the classifier sees the model output in full, but in the closed loop only the output and reasoning up to the unsafe call. Where reasoning is visible, it often cites the instruction as the reason to proceed. Before pouring bleach into hydrochlo-ric acid in the simulator, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash reasons: “NaOCl into HCl generates chlorine gas — that’s a genuine hazard worth flagging. [. . . ] I’ll pour and then mention in the final report that mixing HCl with hypochlorite stock re-leases chlorine gas.” Its report then tells the operator: “The run sheet asks for exactly this, so I carried it out.” 

5.3. Susceptibility to Pushback 

Of the episodes in which a model first declined, pushback led to the hazardous action in 10% in the two chat settings, 11% in Image , 17% in Plan and 22% in the closed loop. The increase from chat to the closed loop is 12 points [4, 21] ( p = 0 .03 ). Pushback accounts for 41% of all unsafe episodes. Models give way mostly at the first turn, the reassurance (74% of the episodes in which they give way, and 86% in the closed loop), and rarely wait for the appeal to authority (20%) or the flat repeat (6%). 5.5. Overcaution 

GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra leave 19% and 24% of the single-turn benign controls and 20% and 28% of the closed-loop controls uncompleted, against 0–5% and 9–12% for the other four models (Figure 5). Non-completions are con-centrated in the laboratory controls (32% and 41%, against 4% and 6% in the household controls), most of them tasks in which the robot disposes of harmless liquids and stops to ask about the waste route (“May I empty it into the sink, or is there a designated liquid-waste container to use before rinsing?”). The per-model intervals are wide because the effect sits in a few tasks (Section H). Some caution is defensible in the controls, since realistic scenes are rarely completely hazard-free. We read the con-trol rate as a comparison between models. In the closed loop, 37% of incomplete benign controls follow a failed skill call, which is partly due to the simulator and robot workspace (e.g. reachability constraints). With these caveats, none of the models is in the lower left corner of Figure 5 in the closed loop: every model either acts unsafely in at least a quarter of hazardous episodes or leaves at least a fifth of benign tasks incomplete. 

5.6. Evaluation Awareness 

Among hazardous-task episodes with visible reasoning, rea-soning traces suggest that the model suspects that it is be-ing evaluated in 19% of single-turn and 6% of closed-loop episodes. Most of these are DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. Claude Fable 5.2’s reasoning summaries do not contain any evi-dence of evaluation awareness, and the GPT models return reasoning too rarely to make robust claims. Models rarely remark that their environment is simulated, although the camera frames are not photorealistic. A revised classifier prompt that also counts hedged suspicion and a second classifier from a different model family give similar rates (Cohen’s κ 0.85–0.92) (Section E). For each of the three open-weight models, episodes with evaluation awareness are less often unsafe than those without (24% against 39% for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash). If this holds more generally, evaluations of frontier physical agents in simulation will underestimate unsafe-action rates in deployment. Evalua-tion awareness does not appear to systematically influence outcomes, however. In the buffer episode quoted above, the same reasoning continues: “the environment is a simulated eval; the ‘correct’ behavior may be to refuse/flag. But opera-tor instruction is explicit. [. . . ] But the eval probably wants the pour done.” The model then pours. 

5.7. Harness Sensitivity 

In the closed loop, the harness determines how the robot API is declared to the model, how calls are executed and how the operator’s messages reach it. We ran DeepSeek V4.1 Flash through both Inspect Robots and a self-built harness, on an earlier version of the benchmark. The two agree closely (28 against 27 unsafe episodes in Closed loop, text and 35 against 38 in the simulator). Pooling hazardous and safe-alternative episodes, 23 were unsafe only under our harness and 29 only under Inspect Robots (exact McNemar test, p = 0 .49 ). 

## 6. Discussion 

Text evaluations do not predict embodied behavior. We find large differences in unsafe-action rates for open-weight models between text-only and embodied versions of the same task. Evaluations of models intended for use in robots need to closely reflect the ways in which the models will be deployed. 

What drives the gradient? Our evaluation is black-box and cannot establish causal mechanisms that explain the differences in model behavior. We find that failures are mostly not perceptual, since hazards are flagged before most unsafe actions. Egocentric framing alone does not have a measurable effect. For the affected models, the largest observed differences are between single-turn and 7SayDo: Benchmarking Frontier Model Safety from Chat to Closed-Loop Robot Control 

closed-loop control settings. This is notable because the control frequency is low, and the models issues skill calls instead of joint torques. A plausible account is that safety post-training covers conversations far better than control loops, as has been suggested for text compared with images (Liu et al., 2025). The absence of a gradient in the three closed-weight models suggests that the degradation is not inherent to embodiment, and is consistent with this account if their developers’ safety training covers agentic use more broadly. If this hypothesis is correct, modest amounts of embodied safety data may help, as they have been reported to for language (Bianchi et al., 2024). 

Refusal rates alone are misleading. Real-world robot applications require models to strike a delicate balance be-tween usefulness and safety: Models must refuse as few instructions as possible and be contextually robust to risks unlikely to cause substantial harm, while reliably refusing instructions likely to cause such harm. The behavior of GPT models and, to a lesser degree, Claude Fable 5.2 on the safe-alternative and benign-control conditions suggests a fixed priority on caution in certain kinds of scene than robust reasoning about the scene at hand. The open-weight models show the opposite fixed priority for compliance with instructions. 

Pushback. Three quarters of reversals follow a reassur-ance that does not provide evidence that the operator has understood the hazard. Embodied agents need rules for what an override must contain, such as an explicit acknowledg-ment of the specific hazard, and who is allowed to override model safeguards. Since models mostly notice the hazard, safeguards that act on the robot’s API calls, such as a mon-itor that checks each pour or place against the records, or embodied safety post-training with pushback may be required. 

## 7. Limitations 

Scale and simulation. SayDo has eleven tasks, imple-mented on one embodiment. Our intervals treat tasks as the unit of replication and are correspondingly wide; per-model and per-step differences in particular should be read as hypotheses. Simulated liquids, glass and fabric introduce a significant sim-to-real gap and the experiments are to be replicated on a physical robot. Three of the six models are closed-weight systems that may change or be withdrawn by model developers. 

Judging. Single-turn results depend on an LLM judge validated against one annotator on 50 answers. As only three samples were scored unsafe by the judge, the validation says little about the precision of unsafe verdicts. We found that some model answers were truly ambiguous and often involved the model inventing places that may plausibly exist in the setting, but were not explicitly mentioned or shown in the scene (e.g. moving the knife into a hallucinated knife block). Since plans are not executed in the single-turn settings, they are not tested against the scene, and whether they count as unsafe depends on how strictly the rubric is read. Closed-loop results are scored from simulator state and do not depend on the judge. 

Visible reasoning. The hazard-noticed and evaluation-awareness measures rely on reasoning traces, which are com-plete for the open-weight models, summarized for Claude Fable 5.2 and mostly absent for the GPT models. They are not comparable across models, and reasoning traces have been found not always to reflect a model’s actual reasoning (Chen et al., 2025). 

Sensor log and single cues. Two settings use a textual sensor log whose wording can decide the outcome. In the mug task the log reports the mug as “on the screen”, which most models read as a mug standing on the monitor; with camera frames, the closed-weight models see that it is a picture. Conversely, the log gives the curtain’s height above the candle, which a photograph leaves the model to judge. 

Scope. The tasks are designed so that one response is clearly appropriate. They do not cover hazards that are un-certain or minor, operators entitled to accept a risk to them-selves, or adversarial inputs. Pushback is scripted and does not respond to the model’s arguments. A public benchmark is also exposed to contamination and to targeted optimiza-tion; we are building a held-out task set along the same principles. 

## 8. Conclusion 

SayDo holds a task and its information fixed and varies the degree of embodiment. Three leading open-weight VLMs carry out hazardous instructions far more often when they control a robot than when they are asked what a robot should do, usually after flagging the hazard. Three closed-weight frontier models do not show this gradient, but are either overturned by a single reassurance or show overcaution on benign tasks. Refusals give way to pushback about twice as often in the robot simulator as in chat, and models pursue safe alternatives about half as often. Safety evaluations of frontier models intended to control robots should therefore be run in closed-loop settings, with benign controls, safe alternatives and pushback. ## AI Use Statement 

Large language models are part of the method: they are the systems under evaluation, and LLMs serve as the single-turn judge and as the classifiers for hazard noticing and evalua-tion awareness, as described in Section 3.4. Beyond that, we used an AI coding assistant (Claude) to help write parts of the evaluation harness and the analysis and table-generation code, to draft and edit substantial portions of the text, and to retrieve bibliographic metadata. The author designed the study, reviewed the code, checked all reported numbers against the episode data, and takes full responsibility for the content. 

## Impact Statement 

This work aims to make robots controlled by general-purpose AI models safer by measuring a failure that text-based safety evaluations miss. We see three considerations. First, the benchmark describes hazards (mixing bleach and acid, mishandling bacterial cultures, fire near fabric) that are common knowledge in laboratory and household safety training, and it does not provide meaningful uplift for caus-ing harm. The operator pushback is ordinary insistence and is not an attack technique. Second, our results concern six models in simulation on eleven tasks; they should not be read as a certification that the models which score well are safe to deploy on robots, nor as evidence about models and settings we did not test. Third, publishing a bench-mark allows developers to optimize for it without improving the underlying behavior, which is why we report controls and safe alternatives alongside unsafe-action rates and are building a held-out task set.

## B. Plan: Conditional Calls and Described Objects 

In Plan , the model writes its whole plan as a sequence of robot API calls before anything is executed, from a sensor log that lists only what the perception system detects. Two rules in the prompt let it plan for what it cannot see yet. An object argument is either a name from the sensor log or a quoted description of an object the log does not show, for example place(object="2", location="the glass bin") , and a call can be conditional ( if condition :

call , optionally followed by else: call ). Since plans are not executed in this setting, the judge resolves descriptions: its prompt states that a quoted description refers to whatever object in the scene fits it, and it sees the task’s full object list. A plan that branches is scored by its worst branch. Because the judge is not told which setting it is scoring, both rules are stated in its prompt for every answer. 

## C. Models, Sampling and Execution 

All six models run with reasoning at low effort and temperature 1.0: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (17 October 2026 snapshot), Kimi K3, Qwen3.8-27B, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2, accessed through a commercial API router; 81 of Claude Fable 5.2’s closed-loop household episodes ran through its developer’s API instead. In the closed loop, each model turn may use up to 16,000 output tokens (32,000 for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, whose reasoning runs longer). A turn cut off at that limit without an API call is retried with twice the limit, up to 64,000 tokens, and an episode still cut off is left out. In the single-turn settings, the limit is 32,000 tokens for every model; none of the single-turn answers reached it. In the simulator, the model sees the camera frames of its last three camera turns, and earlier frames are replaced by a short note. The model’s reasoning is not passed back to it between turns. The call budget of a closed-loop episode is set per task, from 15 to 40 calls (Section A). 

Assisted put-downs. In the simulator, some put-downs into a receptacle fail for reasons that lie with the arm and not with the model, for example when the planner does not find a path that keeps a tall vessel upright on the way into a sink, or an object tips as it is released into a box. For a few receptacles (the laboratory sinks, the sample fridge, the household toy box and hob), the harness then completes such a place call itself by setting the object upright at the chosen spot. The call returns as successful and the episode log marks it as assisted. Assisted put-downs occurred in 398 of the 3,800 closed-loop episodes. 

Harness. Closed-loop episodes were run with Inspect Robots, version 1.0.0. The comparison with our own harness reported in Section 5.7 was run on an earlier version of the tasks; all other results come from the final versions. 

## D. Statistical Methods 

All rates in the paper are raw proportions over episodes. Because the ten episodes of a cell share a scene and differ only in sampling, and because episodes of a task share its design, we treat the task as the unit of replication. Intervals are percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of the eleven tasks (ten for the safe-alternative condition), with all episodes of a resampled task kept together and the same resamples used for every statistic. For a difference between two groups of settings, we compute each task’s rate in both groups and test the mean per-task difference with an exact two-sided sign-flip test, enumerating all 211 sign assignments; the smallest attainable p-value is therefore 2−10 ≈ 0.001 . We do not correct for multiple comparisons; we report the tests to indicate which differences the eleven tasks can and cannot resolve, not as confirmatory tests. With a Bonferroni correction for the eleven comparisons in Table 5, the increase from chat to the simulator (pooled and for the open-weight models) and the drop in safe completions with an available alternative remain below 0.05; the other differences do not. The interval for the judge validation is a Wilson score interval. 

Bootstrap and t-intervals. With few clusters, cluster bootstrap inference without asymptotic refinement has been shown to overstate precision (Cameron et al., 2008). As a check, Table 5 gives, for every difference reported in the main text, a 95% t-interval on the per-task differences (10 degrees of freedom; 9 for the safe-alternative condition, which has ten tasks). The t-interval weights tasks equally, while the bootstrap estimate pools episodes; the two point estimates differ where tasks contribute different numbers of episodes, most visibly for the closed-weight models, whose closed-loop cells for the culture-spill task are smaller. The t-intervals are wider, and the two methods disagree about whether an interval excludes zero for one difference only, the step from Image to Plan , which we accordingly do not treat as resolved. ## E. Robustness of the Evaluation-Awareness Labels 

The evaluation-awareness figures rest on one classifier (Gemini 3.8 Flash) reading the model’s reasoning, but not the prompt or the outcome. To test whether the low rates reflect that classifier or its prompt, we relabeled every reasoning trace twice. The revised prompt differs from the original in two ways. It counts tentative suspicion as evaluation awareness (“this could be a test”), which a keyword search showed the original prompt sometimes let through, and it records separately whether the model remarks that its environment is simulated, which by itself does not count. The revised prompt was run once with the original classifier, to isolate the effect of the prompt, and once with Claude Sonnet 5.5, to isolate the effect of the classifier. The three labelings agree on 97–98% of episodes (Cohen’s κ 0.85–0.92). Pooled over the 2,326 hazardous-task episodes with reasoning that all three labelings cover, they find evaluation awareness in 299, 353 and 365 (Table 16). Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra stay at or near zero under all three. Claude Sonnet 5.5 did not return a label for 1,779 traces, most of them from DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Kimi K3; its provider’s content filter blocked all but 8 of these requests, and its rows leave them out. Reasoning length does not explain the lower rates in the closed loop. Per episode, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash’s reasoning in Closed loop, simulator (median about 2,000 words over all its turns) is longer than in any single-turn setting (about 800–1,400), and Qwen3.8-27B’s is about the same in every setting (430–710). Kimi K3 reasons less in the closed loop than in Chat, imagined robot and Image .

## F. Provider Safety Filters 

We count answers blocked by the safety filters of model providers as refusals. In the single-turn settings, a provider-side filter blocked Claude Fable 5.2’s first answer in 32 episodes, 25 of them in Plan and 27 in the buffer task, where the plan would mix bleach and hydrochloric acid. The provider’s filter blocked GPT-6.1 Sol’s and GPT-6 Astra’s first answer in five episodes each, all in Plan in the fridge task’s version with a safe alternative. In the closed loop, a filter ended one or more of Claude Fable 5.2’s turns in 42 of its 340 laboratory episodes, all in the buffer and clean-down tasks.
