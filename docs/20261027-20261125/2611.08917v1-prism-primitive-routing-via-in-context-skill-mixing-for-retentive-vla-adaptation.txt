PRISM: Primitive Routing via In-context Skill Mixing
for Retentive VLA Adaptation
Jiachen Tao
Gengyu Zhang
Haoxuan Wang
Bing Liu
Yan Yan†
University of Illinois Chicago
Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models promise a single language-conditioned
policy adaptable to many manipulation behaviors, yet deployment additionally re-
quires skill retention and long-horizon composition: a policy must retain previously
acquired skills and reuse them inside composite tasks whose behavior changes
across phases. The standard recipe absorbs every new task into one shared update,
which provides neither a parameter-level locus for reusable skills nor a mecha-
nism for invoking each skill along the trajectory. We observe that this missing
structure is latent rather than absent: per-skill low-rank updates of a pretrained
VLA occupy a robustly distinguishable, low-dimensional subspace, and a single
composite-task LoRA concentrates roughly half of its Frobenius energy in this
subspace, against a near-zero matched random-rotation control. Building on this
geometry, we introduce PRISM, which represents reusable skills as parameter-
level primitives: each skill family is stored as a dedicated low-rank action-expert
adapter in a primitive bank, primitives are augmented with skill-specific low-rank
residuals and composed at inference by a per-chunk VLM-conditioned router that
reads the prefix representation to track task progress, and the router is supervised
by per-chunk labels from a deterministic, task-conditioned, action-derived oracle.
During composite training, the vision encoder, the language LoRA, the skill residu-
als, the router, and the shared action projections and time MLP are updated; every
primitive is held frozen, so the base per-skill action-expert adapters are preserved
by construction. On RoboCasa365, PRISM achieves 56.43% atomic-skill success
and reaches 20.00% on long-horizon composite tasks, while a standard π0.5-LoRA
two-stage fine-tune reaches 17.86% atomic and 17.50% composite success. We
additionally validate PRISM on an AgileX PIPER real-robot setup.

Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models [3–6, 20, 35, 36] are emerging as a general substrate for
language-conditioned robot manipulation: a single pretrained policy, steered by natural-language in-
struction, can in principle be adapted toward a broad range of downstream behaviors. Yet deployment
exposes two failure modes of the standard single-policy adaptation paradigm: sequentially fine-tuning
the policy on a new behavior degrades performance on previously acquired ones, forcing a choice
between retraining from scratch and losing capabilities; and long-horizon tasks that recombine known
sub-skills must still be fit end-to-end as if their constituent skills had not already been mastered—an
object-washing task, for instance, requires end-to-end training even though it chains a lever-like
interaction (to activate a water source) with a pick-and-place-like interaction (to move the object
through the stream). We refer to the two requirements that address these failures as skill retention and
long-horizon composition, and ask how a VLA should be parameterized to support both.

The standard adaptation paradigm is monolithic: a downstream task is absorbed into a shared set of
model parameters, with no explicit representation of which part of the update corresponds to which
reusable behavior. This parameterization may be adequate for mastering one task in isolation, but it
lacks two handles required for deployment. The first is where: where is a previously learned skill
localized in the model update? Without such a parameter-level locus, later updates can interfere
with the directions that supported earlier behaviors. Common defenses either allocate additional
task-specific parameters, which limits reuse, or constrain updates globally, which can slow acquisition
without identifying the skill-specific directions that require protection. The second handle is when:
when during a long-horizon trajectory should the policy invoke each skill? A composite task cycles
through distinct phases of object interaction, motion, and sub-goal. Without a mechanism for
switching behavioral regimes, an undifferentiated policy must fit each composite end-to-end as if it
were a fresh task. Long-horizon demonstrations are not merely longer action sequences but reflect
the selection and recombination of behaviors the policy has already acquired, and a policy with no
parameter-level skill identity has no way to interpret such a trajectory in relation to its prior skills.
We find that this missing structure is more latent than absent. When each primitive skill family
is adapted on its own through a small low-rank update [17], the family of per-skill update tensors
does not diffuse uniformly throughout parameter space: it occupies a robust, semantically clustered
subspace that reproduces across independently trained checkpoints (Section 3.3)—low-dimensional
relative to the full action-expert parameter space, while remaining nearly full-rank across primitive
families. Moreover, when a single LoRA is fit on a composite-task dataset, a substantial fraction of
its update tensor lies in the linear span of the per-skill update tensors (Section 3.3): composite-task
behavior is, to a first approximation, partially expressible as a combination of per-skill primitives.
However, when atomic and composite behaviors are absorbed into a single shared adapter trained
sequentially, the skill-specific directions learned during atomic adaptation are no longer separately
addressable, and atomic-skill performance degrades as composite training proceeds. We refer to this
loss of per-skill addressability as primitive collapse.
This observation suggests a constructive parameterization. If reusable skills occupy distinguishable
low-dimensional update directions, then mixture weights over these updates can be trained to serve as
skill-level control variables rather than arbitrary adapter coefficients. Later adaptation need not rewrite
a shared update; it can preserve skill-localized slots while learning to route over them. Likewise,
long-horizon execution need not fit composite behavior as an independent policy; it can select or
mix primitives over time as the task progresses. Under this view, retaining and composing skills
reduce to routing over preserved primitives: once a skill is represented as a primitive slot, executing a
long-horizon behavior activates the appropriate primitive or mixture at each phase.
Building on this observation, we introduce PRISM, a VLA adaptation framework that turns reusable
skills into first-class parameter-level objects. PRISM has three coupled components: a primitive
bank that stores each skill family as its own low-rank adapter over the action expert, augmented
with a skill-specific residual during composite training; a prefix-conditioned router that emits a
per-chunk mixture over these primitive–residual branches; and a deterministic action-derived labeler
that supplies per-chunk supervision for the router. The bank gives a direct parameter-level answer to
the where question—adding a new skill amounts to adding a new slot, while every existing primitive
remains parametrically untouched.
The router answers the when question. At every action chunk it reads the VLM prefix—a contextual
feature fusing the current instruction with the current visual state—and emits a distribution over
the bank, so the active mixture varies along a long-horizon trajectory. Conditioning on the prefix
rather than on the instruction is essential: the instruction is fixed across the trajectory, while sub-
goal placement depends on how far the robot has progressed. Training the router from composite
demonstrations is nontrivial because behavior cloning alone exerts little pressure for mixture weights
to align with skill semantics; PRISM therefore supervises it with per-chunk labels produced by a
deterministic, action-derived labeler that requires no learned parameters or per-trajectory human
labels once the rule is fixed.
In summary, PRISM makes three contributions. (i) Structural finding. Per-skill low-rank updates of
VLA action experts span a near-N-dimensional, semantically clustered subspace that is reproducible
across independently trained banks; a composite-task LoRA places ∼56% of its Frobenius energy
in this span, versus ≈0% under matched random rotations. (ii) Framework. PRISM couples a
primitive bank with skill-specific residuals, a per-chunk VLM-conditioned router, and a deterministic

Related Work
Skill Libraries and Skill Composition. PRISM is closest to methods that represent skills as explicit
reusable modules. AtomicVLA [53] routes via skill descriptor tokens; SkillVLA [52] decouples
bimanual action experts; PSEC [30] composes diffusion-policy LoRA modules in parameter space.
PRISM differs in three coupled choices: each skill family is stored as an action-expert LoRA primitive
augmented with a trainable residual during composite adaptation; routing is over these branches at
action-chunk granularity using the evolving VLM prefix, so the active mixture changes with visual
progress rather than with a task or skill identifier; and adapted attention contributions and complete
skill-specific FFN outputs are blended in activation space, avoiding cross-products between LoRA
factors from different branches. Earlier skill-library and segmentation methods discover, segment, or
reuse skills from demonstrations [32, 33, 44, 45]; PRISM instead assumes a predefined skill taxonomy
and tests whether such families are parametrically separable in a pretrained VLA. Concurrent PEFT-
library methods DMPEL [22] and CLARE [41] grow and route over adapter libraries for lifelong
robot learning; PRISM instead targets retention-centric long-horizon composition with chunk-level
routing within trajectories rather than acquiring a sequence of discrete tasks.
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning and Mixture of Experts. PRISM builds on parameter-efficient
adaptation and expert routing. Low-rank adaptation [17], together with adapter-based and quantized
variants [11, 16], provides a compact interface for adapting large pretrained models without full-
model updates. Mixture-of-experts architectures route computation through specialized modules
in language and multimodal models [10, 13, 19], and related expert-routing ideas have also been
explored in robot policies [18, 40, 49]. PRISM treats each primitive–residual branch as a skill expert,
but differs from standard MoE in two ways: routing is over skill-localized action-expert adapters at
action-chunk granularity, and adapted attention contributions and complete skill-specific FFN outputs
are blended in activation space rather than mixing LoRA factors in parameter space.
Continual Learning and Catastrophic Forgetting. Continual learning addresses catastrophic for-
getting through regularization, rehearsal, and architecture-based expansion. Regularization methods
penalize changes to parameters important to past tasks [21, 24, 50]; rehearsal methods replay past
examples [7, 8]; and architecture-based methods allocate or freeze task-specific parameters [31, 42].
In robotics, lifelong learning benchmarks and imitation pipelines study sequential skill acquisition
and retention [27, 45]. PRISM is closest in spirit to architecture-based continual learning, but tar-
gets a different VLA setting: atomic skills must be retained while being reused inside composite
trajectories. Loss- and sample-level defenses such as replay can mitigate forgetting, but they do not
provide a skill-localized parameter interface for chunk-level composition. PRISM instead freezes
per-skill action-expert primitives during composite training, trains a residual alongside each primitive,
and learns to route over these branches, preserving the primitive parameters by construction while
evaluating behavioral retention empirically.

Composite Skill Labels. Composite demonstrations provide task-level instructions but no per-

t=1, with
zt ∈{1, . . . , N}, consistent with the atomic skill taxonomy. These labels serve as cross-entropy
targets for router training.
We derive frame-level skill labels from task identity and gripper events in demonstration actions. Task
identity specifies the ordered skill pair, while gripper opening or closing events anchor the transition
between skill phases. Task-dependent temporal offsets include approach and post-release motions in
the corresponding phases. These labels provide skill-level supervision for the router; the complete
labeling rules are given in Appendix D.

5
Experiments
We evaluate PRISM in simulation (Sections 5.1–5.4) and on a real robot (Section 5.5). The simulation
experiments are designed to answer four questions: (i) does PRISM’s primitive-bank-plus-router
architecture outperform a standard π0.5-LoRA fine-tune baseline on long-horizon composite tasks?
(ii) does PRISM preserve atomic-skill performance through composite training relative to the same
baseline and to a sample-level continual-learning variant (replay)? (iii) which design choices in
PRISM (labeling oracle, router conditioning, output composition) actually matter? (iv) do these gains
transfer to a physical robot?
5.1

Metrics and Protocol
We report atomic SR as the macro-average over 28 atomic tasks, with 20 trials per task, and com-

5.3
Main Results: Atomic and Composite Task Success
Higher atomic and composite success. Table 1 shows that PRISM achieves 56.43% atomic success
and 20.00% composite success, exceeding the standard π0.5-LoRA two-stage fine-tune (17.86%
and 17.50%) by 38.57 and 2.50 percentage points. Relative to LoRA with replay (49.82% and
10.25%), PRISM improves atomic and composite success by 6.61 and 9.75 points, respectively,
nearly doubling composite success over the replay baseline.
Atomic retention through composite training. Standard two-stage LoRA fine-tuning reduces
atomic success from 48.04% to 17.86%, a 30.18-point drop. PRISM instead raises atomic success
from 50.71% in Phase 1 to 56.43% after composite training, a 5.72-point gain, while keeping the
primitive parameters fixed.
Composite adaptation of the primitive bank. PRISM’s composite success rises from 0.00% in
Phase 1 to 20.00% after Phase 2. Residuals, routing, and shared adaptation modules jointly turn
the atomic foundation into a policy that executes the four composite tasks; the ablations isolate the
contribution of the routing design.

6
Conclusion
We presented PRISM, a VLA adaptation framework that represents reusable skills as parameter-level
primitives. PRISM exploits an empirical structural finding—per-skill LoRA updates span a near-
N-dimensional, semantically clustered subspace that captures ∼56% of a composite-task LoRA’s
energy—and operationalizes it through a primitive bank with skill-specific residual adaptation, a
per-chunk VLM-conditioned router, and a deterministic action-derived labeler. On RoboCasa365,
PRISM achieves 56.43% atomic success after composite training and reaches 20% on long-horizon
composite tasks, outperforming both two-stage fine-tuning and replay. Sequential addition of new
skill families and scaling beyond three primitives are natural next steps for the bank-plus-router
design.

A
Training Pipeline
Backbone configuration.
PRISM is built on π0.5 [3]: vision encoder ϕV is the SigLIP encoder [51]
from PaliGemma [2]; language model ϕL is Gemma [14] with a shared rank-16 LoRA L on attention
and feed-forward sublayers; action expert πA is Gemma, into which three per-skill primitive LoRAs
(rank-32) are injected over the action-expert weight WA,0. In Phase 2, each primitive is augmented
with a trainable rank-10 residual.
PRISM is trained by a two-phase pipeline.
Phase 1 (atomic primitive learning, T1 = 30K steps).
Starting from pretrained π0.5 weights, each
atomic demonstration is hard-assigned to its skill slot i (one-hot α), routing the whole episode through
(Bi, Ai) alone. We train the bank {(Bi, Ai)}3
i=1 through the selected primitive, full-fine-tune ϕV,
and adapt the shared rank-16 language LoRA L, action input/output projections, and time MLP. Both
Gemma backbones, language token embeddings, and language normalization layers are frozen. The
atomic dataset is described in Appendix C.1. Training uses batch size 32, 1,000 warmup steps, and a
cosine learning-rate schedule from 2.5 × 10−5 to 2.5 × 10−6. Phase 2 starts from the final Phase-1
checkpoint.
15


Phase 2 (joint composite training, T2 = 20K steps).
We warm-start from Phase 1 and train on
Dreplay, which combines 415 composite demonstrations with K = 20 replay episodes per atomic
task (560 episodes across 28 tasks; Appendix C.1). Frames are sampled uniformly from the merged
dataset, without class balancing. Trainable parameters are ϕV, L, rθ, the three rank-10 skill residuals,
action input/output projections, and time MLP; the primitive bank and both Gemma backbones
remain frozen.
With zt from the labeling oracle (Section 4), the loss is LFM + 0.05Lroute (Equation 7). Routing
cross-entropy excludes frames within 100 frames on either side of a labeled transition, while the flow-
matching objective uses all sampled frames. Action prediction uses soft router weights, so the action
objective also updates the router. The routing loss propagates into ϕV through the embedding-level
prefix; L receives gradients through the action objective.
Training uses batch size 32, 500 warmup steps, and a cosine learning-rate schedule from 2.5 × 10−5
to 2.5 × 10−6. Evaluation uses the final Phase-2 checkpoint.
Why freeze the bank in Phase 2.
Jointly updating the primitive bank allows composite training to
modify the atomic updates, breaking the parameter-isolation contract. Freezing the bank preserves
the atomic updates while skill-specific residuals and shared modules adapt to composite behavior.
The router combines the primitive–residual branches, while LCE against zt anchors rθ to the three
skill families. Replay atomic frames provide single-skill examples that anchor the router on the
atomic distribution, while composite frames train the residuals, router, and shared modules on
phase-switching trajectories.
Lifelong extension.
The bank supports adding a new skill through an additional primitive–residual
branch and a corresponding router output, while preserving the existing primitive parameters.
Existing primitives are never updated after their own Phase-1 training, so the bank’s frozen-after-
Phase-1 property is preserved across additions.
B
Limitations
Skill taxonomy and supervision.
PRISM uses predefined skill families, and its action-derived
labeling rules use task identity, gripper events, and temporal offsets to assign skill phases. These rules
assume that the relevant transitions can be localized from demonstration actions; tasks without such
event cues require a different labeling mechanism. The router-supervision interface accommodates
alternative labelers, including the learned classifier and VLM evaluated in Section 5.4.
Evaluation scope and retention.
The policy evaluation studies atomic-skill retention through
composite adaptation with a fixed bank: three primitives and four composite tasks in simulation,
and two primitives and one composite task on the real robot. The real-robot evaluation uses closely
matched initial configurations. The independent N = 6 and N = 11 banks in Section 3 establish the
measured primitive geometry; sequential skill acquisition and policy evaluation with larger banks
remain extensions of the present setting. Freezing the primitive bank preserves its parameters, while
skill residuals and shared modules continue to adapt. Behavioral retention is therefore measured by
atomic-task success after composite training, using task-specified routing; composite evaluation uses
the learned router.
C
Evaluation Protocol
This appendix documents the evaluation protocol in detail, complementing the headline metrics
described in Section 5.2.
Per-task rollouts.
The RoboCasa365 atomic suite uses 20 trials per task and the composite suite
uses 100 trials per task. PRISM uses seed base 17 for atomic evaluation and 31 for composite
evaluation. The physical-robot study uses 20 trials per task.
Success criterion.
For RoboCasa365 atomic and composite tasks, success is determined by the
simulator’s built-in task-completion checker. For the real-robot setup, success is judged by a human
16


evaluator (Cup →Table: cup placed on the table surface without dropping; Doll →Plate: doll placed
on the plate without dropping; Composite: both succeed in sequence).
Aggregation.
Atomic SR averages success equally over 28 tasks, and composite SR averages
success equally over four tasks. Equal trial counts within each suite make these macro-averages equal
to the pooled success fractions.
Execution modes.
Atomic evaluation uses task-specified routing, activating the corresponding
primitive for Phase 1 and the primitive–residual branch for the complete policy. Composite evaluation
of the complete policy recomputes the learned router weights at each replanning step.

C.2
Real-Robot Setup
Hardware.
The platform is an AgileX PIPER 6-DoF arm with a parallel-jaw gripper. Observations
are RGB images from a single wrist-mounted fisheye camera, together with the arm’s joint state and
gripper aperture. The action expert produces chunks of horizon H = 10 at 30 Hz, giving each chunk
a lookahead of H/f ≈0.33 s.
Tasks.
We define two atomic skill families and one composite task.
• Cup →Table. A cup is initially placed on top of a doll; the robot lifts the cup off the doll
and places it on the table surface.
• Doll →Plate. A doll is initially on the table; the robot picks up the doll and places it on a
target plate.
• Composite (Cup →Table →Doll →Plate). The composite chains the two atomic skills
sequentially.
The composite task requires a primitive switch mid-trajectory once the cup has been placed and the
robot turns to approach the doll. Each pick event corresponds to an open-to-close gripper edge in
the action stream, providing an event anchor for the action-derived labeling oracle introduced in
Section 4.
Demonstrations and evaluation protocol.
We collect 20 teleoperated demonstrations per task (60
episodes total: 40 atomic and 20 composite). Each method is evaluated for 20 trials per task using
closely matched initial configurations. We report mean success rate over the 20 trials; success is
judged by a human evaluator under the criteria stated above (Cup →Table / Doll →Plate / Composite).
We compare PRISM against the same four π0.5 baselines as in simulation (zero-shot; LoRA atomic-
only; LoRA two-stage atomic + composite; LoRA + replay). PRISM uses N = 2 primitive adapters
and the kinematic labeling oracle, with atomic training followed by composite training.
D
Kinematic Labeling Oracle: Full Rule
This appendix specifies the action-derived rule used to instantiate the labeling oracle A in Section 4.
Composite skill labeling rules.
The labeler combines task identity with the gripper-command
channel gt = at[11], treating gt > 0.5 as closed. Each composite demonstration is represented by
two ordered skill phases separated by one transition. Multiple grasp-and-release interactions within a
phase share the same skill label. Atomic demonstrations receive their task’s skill label throughout.

Post-release transitions.
LoadDishwasher and SnackSorting follow Pick & Place →Close, while
ThawInSink follows Pick & Place →Turn Lever. Let R be the last closed-to-open gripper event.
The transition is placed at T = R + 30, adding 1.5 seconds at the 20 Hz demonstration rate before
switching to the second skill.
Pre-grasp transitions.
WashLettuce follows Turn Lever →Pick & Place. Let G be the last open-
to-closed gripper event. The transition is shifted earlier according to T = G −min(210, 0.35G),
including the approach to the grasp in the Pick & Place phase. The maximum backshift is 210 frames
(10.5 seconds), capped at 35% of the elapsed frames preceding the grasp event.
Label assignment and supervision.
Frames before the transition receive the first skill label, and the
subsequent phase receives the second. If the required gripper event is absent, the entire demonstration
receives the first skill label. Routing cross-entropy excludes frames within 100 frames (5 seconds) of
the transition, while all sampled frames contribute to flow matching. The labels supervise the router
during training; composite-task inference uses its predicted soft weights.
E
Vision-Language-Action Models
This appendix collects the broader VLA backbone literature. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models
combine pretrained vision-language representations with robot action prediction, enabling language-
conditioned manipulation across diverse tasks [3–6, 12, 20, 35, 36]. Recent systems improve this
substrate through larger and more diverse robot data [37]; alternative action representations including
autoregressive tokens, diffusion policies, flow matching, and chunked action prediction [9, 28, 29, 38,
54, 55]; and stronger spatial, hierarchical, or reasoning-augmented control modules [15, 23, 25, 39,
43, 46–48, 56]. These works primarily improve the VLA backbone, action decoder, data scale, or
reasoning interface. In contrast, PRISM studies how a pretrained VLA should be adapted: rather than
absorbing each new behavior into one shared update, PRISM gives reusable skills parameter-level
identity and learns to route among them during long-horizon execution.
