Abstract
Integrating MLLM-based agents with robotic foundation models has attracted increasing attention
as a means of enhancing robotic capabilities. However, the improvement of such integrated systems
remains largely decoupled across their agent and model components, with advances in one component
not necessarily benefiting the other, substantially limiting the capabilities of the combined system. As
both MLLMs and robotic foundation models become increasingly capable, fully realizing their combined
potential calls for co-evolution, an iterative process in which improvements in agents and robotic foundation
models reinforce one another. We propose RoboCoEvo, a failure-guided co-evolution framework that
couples the refinement of the agent’s explicit and reusable executable knowledge with parameter learning
in robotic foundation models, such as vision-language-action (VLA) models. Through a code-as-policy
formulation, the agent develops and refines executable failure-detection and recovery programs, generating
targeted corrective experience. Post-training consolidates the resulting corrective behaviors into the
robotic model’s end-to-end policy, improving autonomous task execution.
Changes in the updated
policy’s failure distribution, in turn, guide subsequent revisions to the agent’s programs. Experiments on
BEHAVIOR-1K, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin demonstrate sustained improvements in autonomous VLA
task performance across successive co-evolution rounds.

Introduction
Recent work has shown that MLLM-based agentic harnesses can enhance the capabilities of robotic foundation
models [Huang et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026b]. In these works, robotic foundation models provide visuomotor
skills acquired from large-scale demonstrations [Kim et al., 2024, Black et al., 2024, Physical Intelligence et al.,
2025b], while agents support their policy execution through failure diagnosis, recovery, and coordination [Liu
et al., 2023, Lin et al., 2025]. This complementary division of roles enables the combined system to address
limitations of the robotic foundation models. However, the improvement processes of the agent and the
robotic foundation model in the combined system typically remain separate or only loosely coupled [Zhang
et al., 2026a, Yu et al., 2026a], with advances in one component not necessarily benefiting the other. Without
learning from corrective experience, the policy may remain dependent on agent assistance in similar situations.
Meanwhile, an agent whose knowledge is not refined as the policy is updated may continue to target weaknesses
that no longer constrain performance. Sustained improvement therefore requires a co-evolution mechanism
that connects policy learning with the adaptation of agent assistance.
For corrective interventions to yield lasting policy improvements, the policy must incorporate the demon-
strated behaviors into its own capabilities. Agent interventions provide training experience at states where
execution fails, including states induced by accumulated errors and underrepresented in the training distribu-
tion [Ross et al., 2011]. Interactive imitation learning obtains such corrections from human supervisors [Kelly
et al., 2018, Liu et al., 2022], while recent approaches automate recovery and generate corrective data for

the policy to internalize behaviors previously supplied by the agent. As these failures are resolved, however,
the policy’s need for assistance changes, and further improvement requires corrective experience tailored to
its remaining limitations.
On the other hand, acquiring this experience requires the agent to adapt alongside the policy. It must
reassess the updated policy’s failures, determine where intervention is still needed, and revise its recovery
strategies accordingly. Automated failure analysis can support this reassessment [Duan et al., 2025, Yu et al.,
2026b], while a coding agent can use execution feedback to refine the corresponding detectors and recovery
programs. These revisions improve the agent’s assistance and provide relevant corrections for the next policy
update. However, applying conventional code-as-policy approaches without accounting for task-specific failure
characteristics may compromise the accuracy of failure diagnosis and the efficiency of recovery, thereby
limiting subsequent policy improvement. The two directions of improvement therefore form a continuous
feedback loop: the robotic foundation model learns from the agent’s interventions, and its remaining failures
guide what the agent must diagnose and resolve next.
We present RoboCoEvo (Figure 1), a co-evolution framework that couples Policy-Guided Agent
Evolution with Agent-Guided Policy Capability Expansion. The agent evolves through revisions
to executable failure-detection and recovery programs. Guided by policy rollouts and execution feedback,
2


it identifies recurring failure modes, develops and evaluates recovery strategies, and generates corrective
demonstrations across varied task configurations using a code-as-policy formulation [Liang et al., 2022]. The
robotic foundation model then incorporates these corrective behaviors into its end-to-end policy through
post-training. Subsequent evaluation of the updated model identifies residual capability gaps and directs
the next round of program refinement. This iterative exchange improves the agent’s diagnosis and recovery
strategies while expanding the policy’s capacity for independent execution. We instantiate and evaluate
RoboCoEvo with vision-language-action (VLA) models on BEHAVIOR-1K, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin.
Experiments demonstrate sustained improvement across successive cycles, with higher autonomous task
success and stronger generalization to unseen configurations. In summary, our contributions are:
1. Failure-Mode-Centric Agent–Policy Co-Evolution: We introduce RoboCoEvo, a failure-guided
co-evolution framework that couples the refinement of executable agent knowledge with learning-based
policy post-training. Through a recursive feedback loop, the general agent generates corrective experience
to improve the policy, while the evolving policy reveals new capability gaps that guide further agent
adaptation.
2. Policy-Guided Agent Evolution: We develop a failure-mode-centric approach that organizes
recurring policy failures into reusable executable detectors and recovery programs. As the policy evolves,
the agent continuously refines programs for persistent failure modes and develops new ones for emerging
failures, enabling its corrective skills to evolve alongside the policy.
3. Agent-Guided Policy Capability Expansion: We introduce failure-conditioned augmentation
to scale the corrective experience generated by reusable recovery programs. By learning from these
targeted demonstrations, the policy progressively internalizes agent-generated recovery behaviors and
expands its capabilities.
4. Significant Policy Improvement and Data Efficiency: Experiments across BEHAVIOR-1K,
RoboTwin, and RoboCasa demonstrate consistent policy improvements over successive co-evolution
iterations. Extensive analyses further highlight the effectiveness of reusable failure-mode programs and
targeted data augmentation in improving task performance and data-acquisition efficiency.

Related Work
Agent-assisted robotic execution.
LLM- and MLLM-based agents have been widely employed to
enhance robotic execution without modifying the parameters of the underlying policies. Such agents have
been applied to the detection and explanation of execution failures [Liu et al., 2023, Duan et al., 2025, Yu
et al., 2026b, Zhou et al., 2025] and to intervention for failure recovery [Lin et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2026a],
while hierarchical frameworks rely on them to orchestrate low-level policies and skills [Shi et al., 2025, Gemini
Robotics Team et al., 2025, Huang et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026b]. As execution failures are difficult to
anticipate, several systems further refine the agent’s knowledge based on execution feedback, for instance
by revising orchestration code [Huang et al., 2026], by accumulating executable skills [Wang et al., 2024,
Wen et al., 2026], or by updating failure checks and recovery routines for failures grouped according to their
underlying causes [Ding et al., 2026, Sun et al., 2026]. In these systems, however, the acquired improvements
reside in the agent or its programs, whereas any learned policy responsible for low-level control retains fixed
parameters. The capabilities of such a policy therefore remain unchanged, and the agent or its programs
must compensate for its recurring failures at execution time.
Learning from corrective experience.
Policies trained through imitation are prone to compounding
errors that lead them to states underrepresented in their demonstrations [Ross et al., 2011]. Interactive
imitation learning mitigates this distribution shift by training on corrections collected at the states visited by
the policy [Ross et al., 2011, Kelly et al., 2018, Liu et al., 2022, Hu et al., 2025], and RECAP incorporates
such corrections into multi-round training with a learned value function [Physical Intelligence et al., 2025a].
Because these corrections are typically provided by human supervisors, whose effort scales with the number
of failures, recent methods have sought to generate corrective data automatically. IntervenGen and FLARE
extend a small number of human recovery demonstrations to new scene configurations and to new or perturbed
3


failure states [Hoque et al., 2024, Zhao et al., 2026], CARE uses a motion-planning oracle to annotate failure
states sampled from a distribution fitted to failed rollouts [Xiao et al., 2026a], and other approaches derive
corrections from fixed language-model prompts, pretrained experts, privileged continuations, or residual
reinforcement-learning specialists [Bernat et al., 2026, Li et al., 2024b, Snegirev et al., 2026, Xiao et al.,
2025]. These studies demonstrate that training on failure-targeted corrections can improve the policy itself.
Nevertheless, approaches that extend human demonstrations still rely on a supervisor to demonstrate each
new recovery skill, while automatic generators are constructed once and are not refined from the failures they
correct.
Coupled improvement of agents and policies.
The works most closely related to ours couple an
agent with policy training. LRS, VLAMotor, and EmbodiRSI use an MLLM agent or a motion planner to
generate corrections from the failure states of a policy, on which the policy is subsequently fine-tuned [Zhang
et al., 2026c, Liao et al., 2026, Lang et al., 2026]. In these frameworks, however, each failure is corrected in
isolation, so that the knowledge acquired in correcting one failure is neither retained nor refined for similar or
subsequently emerging failures. Other frameworks improve the policy over multiple rounds but differ in how
its corrective behaviors are acquired. Recova learns recovery behaviors with a separate recovery policy rather
than with the task policy [Liu et al., 2026], and RoboClaw retrains its policies on their own rollouts and
on human interventions under a fixed VLM controller [Li et al., 2026]. Robo-COP co-evolves the textual
memory of an orchestrator with a policy but trains this policy exclusively on successful segments of its own
executions, which restricts the learnable behaviors to those that its policy or scripted tools already partially
exhibit [Zhang et al., 2026a]. CoDA co-evolves a data engine with a policy by adapting its scene-generation
and reward-shaping programs to the learning progress of this policy [Yu et al., 2026a], and ENPIRE enables
an agent to revise training recipes [Xiao et al., 2026b]. In both cases, the corrective behavior is still obtained
through the policy’s own exploration or from demonstrations that do not originate at its failure states. SEAR
outlines a comparable agent–policy loop as future work [Wang et al., 2026b]. These frameworks represent
early efforts toward bidirectional improvement between agents and policies. Each, however, realizes only part
of it. In some, the agent corrects failures one at a time without accumulating reusable knowledge of how
to correct them, so that it adapts to individual failures but does not itself improve. In others, the agent
and the policy are both updated, but the policy still learns mainly from its own executions or exploration,
which can limit the recovery behaviors it acquires at the states where it fails. Effective co-evolution therefore
requires that the agent accumulate and refine its knowledge against the policy’s evolving failures, and that
this knowledge be translated into targeted corrective demonstrations for the policy itself at the states where
it fails.

3.2
Policy-Guided Agent Evolution
In each round, the agent adapts its detectors and recovery programs to the failures of the current policy
πi. If the agent instead diagnosed and recovered each failed rollout separately, its cost would grow with the
number of failures, and independent recoveries of similar failures could follow different strategies, leading to
inconsistent demonstrations. RoboCoEvo therefore groups recurring failures into FMs and writes one detector
and one recovery program per FM. The cost of diagnosis and program writing is shared by all failures of an
FM, and all recoveries of an FM follow the same strategy (Section 4.3).
Failure mode identification.
To define a failure mode, we first define the failure pattern Pm as a recurring
pattern in sequences of scene states (stf −h, . . . , stf +ℓ) around the failure time tf, where h and ℓdenote the
numbers of steps before and after the failure, respectively. Similar failure patterns are usually caused by
similar root causes. For example, if a dual-arm robot is holding objects in both hands simultaneously, it
cannot proceed to perform other manipulation tasks. Such cases can recur across different scenarios.
We further define subtasks as successive stages of a task, each with a completion condition expressed in
terms of symbolic facts. For example, a picking subtask is complete when held(k) holds, while a placement
subtask is complete when on(k, k′) holds. A long-horizon task can thus be divided into several subtasks, each
of which can be treated as an independent capability to exercise or goal to achieve.
Hence, a failure mode m is defined as a group of failures that stall at the same subtask and exhibit the
same failure pattern Pm. The failure pattern describes the root cause of the FM and the conditions under
which the policy fails, primarily in terms of facts in Ft and their changes over time.

failure pattern first appears, and a preventive state, an earlier state selected for demonstrating how to avoid
the failure. The FM detector returns variable-size sets of preventive and corrective states:

Recovery program generation.
For each FM, its detected preventive and corrective states are partitioned
into development and held-out validation sets. The agent develops a shared failure recovery program ρm using
simulator states restored from the development set. Following a code-as-policy approach, ρm is implemented as
a finite-state machine over st that composes navigation and manipulation skills with planned motions [Garrett
et al., 2025, Sahoo et al., 2026]. Recovery success is defined as completion of the subtask at which the
original execution failed. The program undergoes iterative refinement and is accepted only when its recovery
success rate reaches at least 70% on the held-out validation set. The recovery programs evolve alongside the
policy. In each subsequent round, the agent develops new programs for newly identified FMs and refines
existing programs for FMs that persist. These refinements aim to broaden the range of failure states that
the programs can address, improve their recovery success rates, and more precisely target the weaknesses
revealed by the current policy.
3.3
Agent-Guided Policy Capability Expansion
The detectors and recovery programs of Section 3.2 are then used to generate corrective experience, on which
the policy is post-trained.
Failure-conditioned augmentation and experience acquisition.
To efficiently generate additional
corrective experience covering the targeted failure mode, we introduce failure-conditioned augmentation,
which creates new failure states by perturbing observed ones in the simulator. This procedure is motivated by
the cost of collecting additional failure instances through rollouts, as each rollout yields only a few instances
of each FM.
For each FM, the agent identifies perturbable components of the scene state, including the robot state
qt, which encompasses the base pose, joint configuration, and end-effector poses, as well as the pose pk
t and
symbolic facts in Ft associated with each task-relevant object k, regardless of whether the object is held.
Additional failure scenes are then generated by perturbing these components while preserving the same failure
mode. Figure 3 shows perturbed failure states on two BEHAVIOR-1K tasks. Perturbed preventive states
must retain the conditions for demonstrating how to avoid the target failure, rather than already exhibit it.
The accepted recovery program ρm is then executed from the detected failure states and from their
perturbed copies without further agent intervention. Only successful recoveries are retained, and together
they constitute the corrective experience Ri of round i. Augmentation thus yields additional corrective
experience from a limited number of observed failures, which reduces the number of rollouts required.

The corrective experience Ri is added to the training data together with the reference
demonstrations, and the policy learns the corrective behaviors through DAgger-style post-training [Ross

4
Experiments
We evaluate RoboCoEvo on three well-known testbeds: BEHAVIOR-1K [Li et al., 2024a], RoboTwin 2.0 [Chen
et al., 2026], and RoboCasa [Nasiriany et al., 2024]. In this section, we first describe our experimental setup,
including benchmark selection, metrics, and training details, in Section 4.1. We then present the main results
of our method to demonstrate its efficiency and performance relative to the base policies. Finally, we conduct
ablation studies to assess the effectiveness of each component of our method.
4.1
Experimental Setup
To comprehensively evaluate our method, we select three well-known simulation-based benchmarks:
• BEHAVIOR-1K [Li et al., 2024a] contains long-horizon, bimanual mobile manipulation tasks in household
scenes and is one of the most challenging benchmarks for robotic manipulation. We use its first six
tasks, covering the most common household activities.
• RoboTwin 2.0 [Chen et al., 2026] covers bimanual tabletop manipulation with diverse coordination
requirements. We evaluate on all 50 tasks.
• RoboCasa [Nasiriany et al., 2024] provides everyday manipulation tasks in realistic and diverse kitchen
environments, using a single-arm mobile manipulator. We select ten tasks from different task categories,
covering the benchmark’s most representative task types. The selected tasks are listed in Appendix A.

episode. All policies are evaluated on the official test instances, with 50 instances per task on RoboTwin and
RoboCasa. On BEHAVIOR-1K, we run each of the 20 test instances per task twice. RoboCoEvo improves a
VLA that executes tasks on its own, so we compare each updated policy with its base policy under the same
evaluation protocol.
Data generation and training.
In each round, we collect rollouts with the current policy using seeds
distinct from those used for training and official testing. On BEHAVIOR-1K, we collect 50 rollouts per
task and sample 20 failures. On RoboCasa and RoboTwin 2.0, we collect 200 rollouts per task to obtain
a sufficient number of failures, as the base policies achieve higher success rates on these benchmarks than
on BEHAVIOR-1K. For each FM, the agent writes a detector and a recovery program (Section 3.2). The
execution framework described in Section 3 then applies the detectors to all failed rollouts, runs the recovery
programs on the detected failure states, and uses the agent to perturb these states for failure-conditioned
augmentation (Section 3.3). We use GPT-6 Astra with high reasoning effort as the agent. During fine-tuning
of the VLA, we sample the original demonstrations and the accumulated failure experience at a 1:1 ratio to
mitigate catastrophic forgetting. The training hyperparameters are provided in Appendix B.
4.2
Main Results
Overall, RoboCoEvo improves the base policies across all three benchmarks, as shown in Table 1. After two
rounds, the average success rate rises from 79% to 93% on RoboTwin and from 79% to 91% on RoboCasa, and
the mean Q-score on BEHAVIOR-1K increases from 0.40 to 0.56. These results show that failure-targeted
experience can translate into improved full-task performance across different manipulation settings.
The gains develop over successive rounds of experience acquisition and policy updates. Both rounds
improve the policy on every benchmark, and the first round yields the larger gain in success rate. These
multi-round gains demonstrate RoboCoEvo’s ability to improve through successive policy updates. As the
policy becomes more capable, continued trial and error reveals remaining weaknesses and provides new targets

We also report per-task results on BEHAVIOR-1K to illustrate our method’s performance across different
tasks in Table 2. The results show steady Q-score improvements across both rounds on most tasks. On

gradually improves to 0.03 and then to 0.09. These small gains understate the progress: as the policy takes
the jar out of the cabinet more reliably, more rollouts reach the later subtasks of opening the jar and fetching
the sausage, where new failure modes are exposed (Appendix E). Overall, these results support RoboCoEvo’s
ability to improve policies over multiple rounds, with the pace and extent of improvement varying according
to the task and the policy’s existing capabilities.
To understand these gains, we apply the failure-mode detectors to every test episode of each policy
(Table 1). The base and first-round policies are evaluated using first-round detectors, and the second-round
policy using second-round detectors. For each policy, we report the fraction of episodes in which a targeted
failure mode occurs (FM occurs), the fraction of occurrences from which the policy recovers and completes
the step without assistance (Self-recovered), and the fraction of episodes with an occurrence in which the
failure mode is never recovered and the episode fails (Fail | FM ). Failed episodes are further divided into
those that fail with an unrecovered targeted failure mode (Targeted FM ) and those that fail without one
(Unexplained). Following the first round, the targeted failure modes occur less frequently, and the updated
policy is more likely to recover autonomously when failures arise. On RoboTwin, the share of occurrences the
policy recovers from rises from 0.18 to 0.54, and the fraction of episodes that fail by a targeted failure mode
drops from 0.19 to 0.06. The policy has therefore learned to get out of these failures, not only to avoid them.
Figure 4 shows one example per benchmark: the base policy stays in its failure, while the first-round policy
enters the same kind of failure and recovers on its own. A per-failure-mode breakdown of these results is
provided in Appendix E.
The second-round experiment further highlights the importance of adapting detectors and recovery
programs to each new policy (Table 3). When the first-round programs are applied without modification
to the failed rollouts of the first-round policy, the detection success rate falls from at least 94% to between
54% and 72%, and fewer than half of the failure states are detected and recovered. After the agent revises
the programs using these rollouts, detection success returns to its first-round level, while recovery success
increases to 71%–83%. The revised detectors also account for more of the failures of the second-round policy
on the test episodes. On RoboTwin, for instance, the fraction of episodes that fail without an unrecovered
targeted failure mode decreases from 0.040 for the first-round policy to 0.017 for the second-round policy.
These results indicate that the policy’s failure distribution shifts as it improves, such that programs developed
for an earlier version no longer cover its current failures. Revising these programs in each round keeps the
corrective experience targeted at the current policy’s failures.

Ablations
We ablate the components of RoboCoEvo on the task picking up trash in BEHAVIOR-1K (Table 4). This
task is selected as a representative task that consists of long-horizon, repeated skills and in which multiple
failure modes occur. As illustrated in Table 4, we compare variants trained from the same RLC checkpoint
using different training data compositions. The variants differ in whether all failures of an FM share one
detector and one recovery program (FMs) and in whether failure states are multiplied by failure-conditioned
augmentation (Aug.). Success-only data, which consists of successful demonstrations that do not target
failures, serves as an untargeted control. All variants generate recovery data with the same agent and with
approximately the same total length of 9 to 10 h, matching the total length of the original training data
at a 1:1 ratio. Each fine-tuned policy is then evaluated on 40 test episodes. Appendix C describes the
implementation of the variants and the cost accounting.
Targeted versus untargeted data.
Full RoboCoEvo increases the Q-score of the base policy from
0.633 to 0.84, whereas Success-only data only reach a Q-score of 0.642 while requiring 72.4 GPU hours and
approximately 16 hours of human work. Recovery data targeted at the failures of the policy are therefore
more effective than a comparable amount of untargeted demonstrations, and they require half the GPU hours
and no human effort. The importance of data thus comes not only from diversity but, most importantly,
from distributions focusing on the weaknesses where the policies are likely to fail.
Shared failure-mode programs.
Per-failure recovery, in which a separate agent localizes and recovers
each failed rollout with its own program, reduces the Q-score of the base policy to 0.267. We attribute this
degradation to the inconsistency of the resulting demonstrations, which motivates the shared FM programs
of RoboCoEvo (Section 3.2). BEHAVIOR-1K tasks require high-dimensional mobile manipulation, in which
a 6-D end-effector pose can be reached by many configurations of the 7-DoF arm, the 4-DoF trunk and the
mobile base. Independent agents relying on motion planners therefore admit many different solutions for

Failure-conditioned augmentation.
The main benefit of augmentation is a lower simulation cost. In
per-failure recovery with augmentation, the agent adapts the program written for each failure to several
perturbed copies of its failure state, which requires fewer tokens than solving a new failure from scratch.
The same amount of data then requires far fewer failures and thus fewer rollouts, which reduces agent token
usage by a factor of six and simulation by a factor of 3.6. The saved time comes from the fact that, without
augmentation, the FM detectors must process a much larger set of rollouts to collect enough failure states,
whereas full RoboCoEvo obtains its failure states from 50 rollouts and perturbs them, reducing simulation
time from 147 to 36 GPU hours. Augmentation also improves the resulting policies, raising the Q-score from
0.267 to 0.483 for per-failure recovery and from 0.758 to 0.84 with FM programs. Since the perturbed states
of a failure are recovered by the same program or an adaptation of it, their recoveries remain similar and the
data become more consistent. These results indicate that, on a high-dimensional mobile manipulator, such
consistent data facilitate the learning of complex skills.

5
Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we presented RoboCoEvo, a failure-guided co-evolution framework that couples the refinement
of an agent’s executable knowledge with the post-training of robotic foundation models. In each round,
the agent identifies the recurring failure modes of the current policy and develops or refines a reusable
detector and recovery program for each mode. By combining these detectors and recovery programs with
failure-conditioned augmentation, RoboCoEvo generates corrective experience targeted at the current policy’s
weaknesses. Post-training on this experience expands the capabilities of the policy, and the failures of the
updated policy in turn guide the next round of agent refinement. Experiments on BEHAVIOR-1K, RoboTwin,
and RoboCasa show that RoboCoEvo improves task performance over successive co-evolution rounds starting
from strong initial policies and reduces the frequency of failures caused by targeted failure modes. Ablation
studies further evaluate the contributions of individual components to policy performance and data acquisition
efficiency.
Limitation.
Currently, RoboCoEvo learns only from successful recovery demonstrations, while the
VLA’s own successful and failed rollouts are not used for training. Future work could combine RoboCoEvo
with reinforcement learning to leverage these rollouts, improving data utilization through outcome-based
credit assignment.

B
Training Parameters
BEHAVIOR-1K.
We initialize each task from the released per-task checkpoint of Robot Learning Collective,
the first-place solution of the 2025 BEHAVIOR Challenge, and each round fine-tunes all parameters of the
previous round’s policy. Each round trains for two epochs or 30,000 steps, whichever is shorter, with a global
batch size of 128, sampling the task’s 200 human demonstrations and the accumulated recovery data at a 1:1
ratio. We use AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.95), a weight decay of 10−10 and gradient clipping at 1.0, with an
EMA decay of 0.99. The learning rate is warmed up linearly over 1,000 steps to a peak of 5 × 10−5, followed
by cosine decay to 10−6. Action targets are normalized with the base checkpoint’s statistics and clipped to
±10.
RoboTwin.
We initialize π0.5 from the checkpoint trained on both clean and randomized RoboTwin
demonstrations for round 1 and from round 1’s final EMA checkpoint for round 2, resetting the optimizer.
Each round fine-tunes all parameters for 40,000 optimizer updates with a global batch size of 128, sampling
original demonstrations and accumulated recovery data at a 1:1 ratio. We use AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.95), a

peak of 2 × 10−5, followed by cosine decay to 2 × 10−6.

Ablation Details
Data budget and variants.
We use picking up trash (BEHAVIOR-1K task 1), in which the robot must
place three soda cans into a trash can. All variants start from the RLC checkpoint and draw their failures
from its rollouts on this task, excluding the 20 public test instances. Each variant uses only as many of these
rollouts as it needs to generate its data, and its GPU cost is computed from the rollouts it actually uses.
Each variant generates an amount of new data comparable to the original demonstrations (9.76 h), and these
data are sampled 1:1 with the original demonstrations during fine-tuning. Apart from the ablated component,
the pipeline is identical to that of the main experiments. Failure states are restored from the simulator states
recorded in the rollouts, and recovery programs act through the action space of the robot using classical
methods, namely inverse kinematics, interpolation, navigation and closed-loop correction. The variants are as
follows (FMs: shared failure-mode programs, Aug.: failure-conditioned augmentation).
• Success-only data. Approximately 5 h of successful rollouts of the base policy and 5 h of demonstrations
collected by human teleoperation, which cost 72.4 GPU hours and approximately 16 hours of human
work, respectively. These data follow the original data distribution and do not target failures.
• Per-failure recovery. Each failed rollout is handled by a separate agent, which identifies the failed
subtask, restores start states shortly before or after the error, and writes a program that completes
this subtask. A recovery succeeds when the goal condition of the subtask is satisfied. Neither FMs nor
augmentation are used.
• Per-failure recovery with augmentation (Aug.). Per-failure recovery on 50 randomly chosen
failures, whose failure states are multiplied by failure-conditioned augmentation. The agent adapts the
program of each failure to its perturbed states, which requires fewer tokens than solving a new failure
from scratch.
• FMs without augmentation (FMs). The five FMs of full RoboCoEvo on this task and their recovery
programs, with detectors implemented from the same FM specifications. The detectors locate the
preventive and corrective states of each FM in the failed rollouts, and the recovery program of the FM
is executed on these states without augmentation.
• Full RoboCoEvo (FMs + Aug.). The first round of the main experiments on this task (Section 3):
five FMs discovered from 50 rollouts, their detectors and recovery programs, and failure-conditioned
augmentation of the detected states.
Cost accounting.
Tokens are the sum of input, cache-write, cache-read and output tokens over all agent
sessions of a variant and over the orchestrating session that launched and monitored them. A variant that
reuses programs of full RoboCoEvo also includes the tokens of the agents that wrote them. GPU hours
include only the simulation that produces data,
G = Groll + Grec,
Groll = Nroll ¯t,
where Groll is the cost of the base-policy rollouts that a variant uses to find its failure states, Nroll is the
number of these rollouts, ¯t = 0.25 is the average number of GPU hours per rollout, and Grec is the cost of

the programs reused from full RoboCoEvo, are counted once. Recording time is additionally converted to the

Failure-Mode Metrics
The failure-mode columns of Table 1 are computed over the test episodes E of a policy on a benchmark, of
which F ⊆E fail. Occurrences and recoveries are determined by the agent-written detectors on the recorded
scene states: an occurrence counts as recovered if the policy, without assistance, later completes the subtask
in which it occurred, i.e., the subtask’s completion condition (Section 3.2) holds before the episode ends. A
failure mode is unrecovered in an episode if it occurs and the policy never recovers from it. The base and
first-round policies are evaluated using first-round detectors, with targeted FMs defined as those that received
recovery data in that round. The second-round policy is evaluated using revised and new detectors, alongside
first-round detectors for unchanged FMs. We report the following failure analysis metrics:
• FM occurs = |Eocc|/|E|, where Eocc is the set of episodes in which at least one targeted FM occurs.
• Self-recovered = R/N, where N is the number of occurrences of targeted FMs over all episodes and R
the number of those the policy recovers from on its own.
• Fail | FM = |Ftgt|/|Eocc|, where Ftgt ⊆F is the set of failed episodes with an unrecovered targeted FM.
• Targeted FM = |Ftgt|/|E|.
• Unexplained = |Fnone|/|E|, where Fnone = F \ Ftgt is the set of failed episodes with no unrecovered
targeted FM.

Per-Failure-Mode Analysis
Table 1 aggregates the targeted failure modes (FMs) of each benchmark. Figures 5–8 break this aggregate
down by FM, using the same detectors and counting rule. We compare the policies before and after each
round of co-evolution. For example, after the first round of co-evolution, we compare the base policy with the
first-round policy under the detectors written in the first round. For each FM, we report the fraction of its
task’s test episodes that fail with this FM never recovered from. This rate falls when the policy avoids the
FM and also when it recovers after the FM occurs. FMs that cause no failure under either policy are omitted.
In the first round, failure rates decrease for 84 of the 98 FMs on RoboTwin, 29 of the 48 on RoboCasa,
and 14 of the 22 on BEHAVIOR-1K. Among the FMs that cause at least 5% of their task’s episodes to fail
under the base policy, the corresponding counts are 61 of 66, 16 of 20, and 13 of 20, respectively. The largest
reductions occur for the FMs that dominate the base policy’s failures: “hovering over the bell without pressing
it” in click bell on RoboTwin (69% to 0%), “stall at the faucet lever” in WashFruitColander on RoboCasa
(14% to 0%), and “holding the traps without releasing them” in setting mousetraps on BEHAVIOR-1K (22.5%
to 2.5%).
In the second round, failure rates decrease for 50 of the 86 FMs on RoboTwin and 19 of the 29 on
RoboCasa. Among FMs meeting the same 5% threshold under the first-round policy, the corresponding
counts are 20 of 27 and 8 of 9, respectively. The largest reductions occur for “hanging the mug with its
handle up” in hanging mug on RoboTwin (37% to 22%), “knob slipping during turning” in TurnOffStove on
RoboCasa (17% to 6%), and both “wandering instead of picking the ice” and “dropping the ice cube off the
glass” in MakeIceLemonade (each 6% to 0%). On BEHAVIOR-1K, the largest second-round reductions occur
18


for “stalling with full hands at the shut drawers” in putting away Halloween decorations (60% to 30%) and
“pressing the button while it faces away” in turning on a radio (30% to 15%).
In Figure 8, the FMs of canning meat remain frequent after both rounds, but the FMs of the earlier
subtasks decline in each round, namely the jar-grasping failures in the first round and the lid-opening failure
in the second, while the failures of the subsequent subtasks become more frequent. Canning meat shows how
failures move to later subtasks in a long-horizon task. Its subtasks follow a fixed order: open the cabinet,
take the jar out, open the jar’s lid, fetch the sausage, and put it into the jar. Table 7 groups the episodes by
the furthest subtask they reach. Under the base policy, 51% of episodes never get the jar out of the cabinet.
After Round 1, this falls to 12%, and opening the lid, which more episodes now reach, is exposed as the main
bottleneck: 40% of episodes get the jar out but never open the lid, up from 24%. After Round 2, only 3%
of episodes stay at the jar and 18% at the lid, while 79% open the lid and reach the sausage, up from 24%
under the base policy; fetching the sausage becomes the next bottleneck. Each round thus moves the policy
one subtask further. Because the Q-score credits only goal conditions satisfied at the end of an episode, it
reflects this stage-wise progress only partially (0.02 to 0.09).
