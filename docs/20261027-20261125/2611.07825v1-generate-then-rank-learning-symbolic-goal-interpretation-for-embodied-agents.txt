Generate-then-Rank: Learning Symbolic Goal
Interpretation for Embodied Agents
Jisen Lei and Huizhi Liang
School of Computing, Newcastle University, Newcastle upon Tyne, United Kingdom
leijisena@gmail.com
Abstract. Goal interpretation is to translate a natural language instruction into
a formal symbolic goal that a planner can execute. It is critical for embodied
agents because any error here propagates to every downstream planning and ac-
tion step. Doing it well requires both generating suitable candidates and reliably
selecting among them. We present a two-stage generate-then-rank approach to
Goal Interpretation in the Embodied Agent Interface (EAI). First, supervised
fine-tuning adapts a Large Language Model (LLM) to EAI’s output convention.
Second, Candidate-Output Ranking from Errors (CORE) learns preferences over
complete outputs from the model’s own generated alternatives. We conducted ex-
periments on the household scenes of the Embodied Agent Interface Benchmark.
Multiple open source LLMs including DeepSeek-V3 chat, DeepSeek-Reasoner,
and Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-72B were evaluated. The proposed method out-
performed the baseline models including LLMs with simple prompting, chain-of-
thought, and knowledge graph based approaches.
Keywords: Embodied AI · Goal interpretation · Large language models · Candi-
date ranking.
1
Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) increasingly connect natural-language instructions to
embodied planning and action [1, 12, 16]. Before planning, however, an agent must de-
termine what terminal conditions constitute success. Goal interpretation is to translate a
natural language instruction into a formal symbolic goal that a planner can execute. It is
critical for embodied agents because any error here propagates to every downstream plan-
ning and action step. The Benchmark Embodied Agent Interface (EAI) [5] formalises
this goal interpretation task as a mapping from a VirtualHome scene and instruction to
object states, directed relations, and terminal actions [5, 7]. An omitted condition, un-
intended relation, or incompatible predicate argument can make downstream behaviour
incomplete or infeasible even when the planner is otherwise correct.
For example, the instruction “wash dishes with dishwasher” in Fig. 1 requires the
dishwasher to be CLOSED and ON and the directed relations ON(plate, dishwasher),
ON(cup, dishwasher), and ON(dish_soap, dishwasher). Walking to the kitchen
or opening the dishwasher is an intermediate step, not a terminal goal. A goal that
omits ON(dish_soap, dishwasher) is still well formed, yet it is incomplete. A rela-
tion is also directed: ON(plate, dishwasher) is correct, whereas ON(dishwasher,

plate) is wrong. Well-formed output alone therefore does not ensure correct goal

Direct generation provides a useful baseline [5], but the single output a model
returns is not necessarily the best goal it can generate: sampling several outputs can
yield a more complete goal. Task-specific supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can adapt an
LLM to the required output format and sparse terminal-goal convention [8, 4]. SFT alone,
however, optimises reference-token likelihood rather than an explicit preference between
complete candidate goals. Sequence-level ranking has addressed related mismatches
in text generation [6, 17]. Our candidate analysis motivates studying this mismatch
for symbolic goal interpretation: a better goal may already be generated but remain
unselected.
Our approach comprises two learned stages, shown in Fig. 1. Stage 1 is supervised
fine-tuning for goal generation: it takes the scene and instruction and produces several
complete candidate goals. Stage 2 is learning to rank candidate outputs, which we call
Candidate-Output Ranking from Errors (CORE): it takes the input and its candidate pool,
assigns every candidate a sequence score, and outputs the highest-scoring goal. CORE is
learned rather than rule-based. During training it compares the model’s own better and
worse candidates and adjusts the scorer so that higher-quality complete Node–Edge–
Action outputs receive higher scores, while a supervised anchor retains the reference
target. Its contribution is the application of error-derived candidate ranking to symbolic
goals, rather than a claim to invent supervised or preference learning.
Our main contributions are as follows: (i) a two-stage generate-then-rank approach
that combines generation and ranking to improve goal interpretation with large language
models; (ii) a training procedure that first applies supervised fine-tuning and then learns
to rank candidates from the model’s own errors with a contrastive objective; and (iii)
experiments on the household scenes of the Embodied Agent Interface benchmark,
comparing several LLMs and baselines that include supervised fine-tuning, candidate-
level preference optimisation, and knowledge-graph-based methods.
2
Related Work
Embodied-language systems ground instructions in affordances, generate action pro-
grams, and interleave reasoning with interaction [1, 12, 16]. EAI instead isolates four
interfaces: goal interpretation, subgoal decomposition, action sequencing, and transi-
tion modelling [5]. Goal interpretation predicts sparse terminal Node, Edge, and Action
records rather than a trajectory. This distinction makes plausible intermediate actions
false positives and requires exact relation direction, cardinality, and stopping.
Autoregressive likelihood scores token sequences, whereas EAI evaluates the parsed
final set. Candidate-ranking methods such as BRIO and SLiC-HF calibrate complete
outputs with preference and supervised signals [6, 17]; DPO learns preferences relative
to a frozen reference policy [9]. Cal-DPO additionally calibrates implicit reward magni-
tudes [14]; CORE instead uses reference-derived quality gaps to scale pairwise margins.
CORE applies this sequence-level perspective to complete symbolic goals, using re-
alised model errors and an SFT anchor. Related planning research also shows the value
of structured environmental evidence [10, 15]; we therefore include knowledge-graph-

[5]; ranking among complete candidate goals is not part of that protocol, which is the

Sampling and self-consistency offer another way to use multiple model outputs [13];

3.3
Stage 2: Learning to Rank Candidate Outputs
Why rank candidates Greedy decoding takes the most probable token at every step.
Each choice is locally best, but the resulting complete goal is not necessarily the best
goal the model can produce: one early token, such as the source of an Edge, fixes the
rest of the output. Sampling several complete outputs exposes alternatives, and our
candidate analysis (Section 5) finds items whose pool contains a better goal than the
one selected. Stage 1, however, is trained only to reproduce reference tokens; nothing
teaches it to score a better complete candidate above a worse one. Stage 2 therefore adds
a learning-to-rank step. Unlike a fixed scoring rule, its ranking function is optimised
from data.
Input, output, and components As shown in the Stage 2 part of Fig. 1, the input is
𝑥𝑖together with its candidate pool, and the output is one selected complete goal ˆ𝑦𝑖.
The only component is a scorer: the Stage 1 model, whose LoRA parameters continue
to be trained. No separate ranking network or classification head is added. Training
changes the scorer with the CORE loss below; inference applies the trained scorer to
each candidate and returns the highest-scoring one without reading any reference.

checkpoint produces 𝐾= 9 outputs: one greedy completion and two groups of four
sampled completions. Exact duplicate strings are removed, yielding candidate pool C𝑖
with 1 ≤𝑀𝑖≤𝐾unique complete Node–Edge–Action goals. Deduplication occurs
before scoring, so repeated samples have no voting advantage.

contains three unique candidates, which differ only in their edge goals. Candidate 1,
the greedy output, omits the dish_soap relation (Combined F1 0.889, Edge F1 0.800);
Candidate 2 also omits cup (0.750, 0.500); Candidate 3 matches the reference (1.000,

likelihood selection already returns the complete goal that greedy decoding misses. After
CORE training the scores are −0.0173, −0.0236, and −0.0139: the weakest candidate
falls, the complete one rises, and the selection is unchanged. As average per-token

and 98.58% before and 98.28%, 97.67%, and 98.62% after. CORE reranks the same
archived candidates; it does not regenerate them. One record illustrates the mechanism
only; Table 4 gives the 68-record scores, where the Edge component is lowest throughout.
No reference is read at inference; F1 is computed afterwards. During training the same
construction is applied to training records only: candidates are model-generated, scored
against training references, and paired when their quality differs. The ranking term raises
the preferred candidate’s relative sequence score while the SFT term retains the reference
target. CORE thus ranks complete candidates and selects one, rather than classifying
the input into a fixed set of task labels.

Experiments
4.1
Dataset and Split
The study uses the 342 public Goal Interpretation records from EAI [5], whose household
scenes derive from VirtualHome [7]. Each record contains an instruction, the relevant
scene and schema, and a sparse reference goal with Node states, directed Edge relations,
and terminal Actions. The target represents success conditions, not an action sequence.
Prompts and reference semantics are retained unchanged; identifier, parsing, duplication,
and split-conservation checks verify the materialised corpus.
Task families are grouped before splitting. Twenty-one families (274 records) form
the adaptation set; five families (68 records) form the diagnostic set. Table 2 reports
their composition.

Parameter Settings
SFT adapts Qwen2.5–7B-Instruct [8] with LoRA for three epochs, learning rate 2×10−4,
and seed 42; only assistant tokens contribute to the loss. Candidate texts and reference-
derived training utilities are fixed before preference training; model likelihood scores are
recomputed and updated. CORE continues SFT for one epoch with learning rate 10−5,
gradient accumulation eight, 𝛼= 𝛾= 1.000, and 𝑚= 0.100, producing 73 comparisons
and 35 updates. DPO uses 41 aligned pairs, one epoch, learning rate 5×10−6, 𝛽= 0.100,
and the same diagnostic exclusion.
The archived search configuration specifies task-grouped five-fold search within the
274 adaptation records, with 𝛾∈{0.03, 0.10, 0.30, 1.00} and 𝑚∈{0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20}
in a staged search. The final run verifies 𝛾= 1 and 𝑚= 0.10; detailed fold scores are
not available in the retained archive, so we do not claim a measured sensitivity curve.
The anchor weight 𝛼= 1, Edge utility weight 0.25, and gap floor 0.01 are fixed design
choices, not independently validated optima. The Edge term prioritises relational errors;
the floor supplies a minimum positive quality gap.
The fixed diagnostic bank was generated by an earlier ranking continuation (rank
weight 0.10), not the pure SFT checkpoint. It uses one greedy output and four samples at
each of two temperature and top-𝑝settings, (0.65, 0.90) and (0.90, 0.95), with a 2048-
token limit and exact-string deduplication. CORE reranks this same archived bank. This
controls candidate availability for that reranking contrast, but does not isolate the source
of the bank or end-to-end training effects against greedy SFT. Our inference ablation
keeps the earlier checkpoint fixed. A shared bank from the exact SFT parent would be
needed for a matched selector study; separate banks per selector would instead measure
complete generation-and-selection pipelines.

4.4
Comparison Methods
We report DeepSeek-V3 Chat [3], a directly prompted LLM, and DeepSeek-Reasoner,
which reasons step by step before answering, as full-corpus, no-closure reproductions.
Archived raw Qwen2.5–7B and 72B inference results provide an additional model-
family reference under the original EAI protocol. These models are evaluated without
additional training; their 342-item scope differs from the adapted-model diagnostic.
The 68-item configuration comparison contains greedy SFT, length-normalised like-
lihood ranking with the earlier ranking-trained checkpoint, candidate-level DPO, and
our SFT+CORE approach. All use the same diagnostic identifiers and original EAI
evaluator. BRIO and SLiC-HF motivate sequence-level ranking but are not additional
reproduced baselines [6, 17].
KG-based comparators We include two distinct knowledge-based routes. The unadapted
Qwen2.5–7B KG-prompt arm prepends scene-relevant schema hints (object states, rela-
tion constraints and implications) to the task prompt, without SFT or CORE. We score
its archived raw response, without the older pipeline’s deductive closure or refinement,
to isolate this prompt-only configuration. The Euclidean and hyperbolic KG arms in-
stead combine standard SFT with projected KG prefix representations, without CORE
ranking. Their archived runs use the same 274/68 task split, three epochs, learning rate
2×10−4 and seed 42. They are KG-enhanced language models, not standalone symbolic
KG solvers. We re-evaluate all archived outputs on the same 68 identifiers using the
current original-EAI resources; re-evaluation reproduces the SFT baseline. These are
configuration comparisons with differing training and inference procedures, not isolated
causal estimates of KG utility.

Results and Discussion
5.1
Large-Model and Adapted-Qwen Results
The reasoning-specialised model improves the unassisted score from 0.318 to 0.434.
Additional reasoning therefore helps, but does not by itself solve EAI’s sparse symbolic
representation problem. Since model scale, training data, and reasoning procedure are
confounded, this is not a causal scaling claim.
Table 3 reports full-corpus model results separately from the adapted-model com-
parison. Raw Qwen2.5–72B obtains 0.293 Combined F1 in the archived original-EAI
evaluation, providing a second model family alongside DeepSeek. Different evalua-
tion protocols prevent a controlled cross-family ranking. Likewise, raw full-corpus and
adapted diagnostic scores do not quantify a matched SFT effect.
Table 4 compares adapted models on the same 68-item diagnostic. CORE improves
Combined F1 by 0.020 and Edge F1 by 0.035 over greedy SFT. Its 0.001 Combined
advantage over DPO is descriptive; one seed and a repeatedly inspected diagnostic do
not support a significance claim. DPO remains strongest on Action F1.

5.2
Comparison with KG-Based Methods
Table 5 compares the archived KG configurations under one diagnostic/evaluator scope.
KG prompting scores below its unadapted baseline, while both KG-prefix SFT arms
score below plain SFT. CORE has the highest Combined and Edge F1 among these
configurations. This result concerns the implemented knowledge interfaces and does
not establish that KGs are ineffective in general. In particular, unadapted prompting and
trained ranking use different supervision budgets.
5.3
Candidate Selection Analysis
For the earlier ranking-trained checkpoint, length-normalised sequence ranking changes
12/68 greedy selections and raises all four components. Candidate sets contain 1–9
unique outputs (mean 3.353). An Edge-level analysis partitions the items into 38 exact
selected sets, 11 generation failures with no improved candidate, and 19 recoverable
selection failures. A better Edge candidate lies within the sequence-score top three for
16/19, but the selector chooses an improvement in only 6/19. The 0.097 Combined gap
between sequence ranking and the gold-selected oracle locates substantial unrealised
information in the candidate bank. The non-deployable oracle reaches 0.823 Combined
(0.957 Node, 0.752 Edge, 0.692 Action); it is an analysis upper bound, not an experi-
mental method.
The analysis suggests different interventions: improve candidate generation when no
better goal is available, and improve ranking when it is already present.
5.4
Ablation: Candidate-Output Ranking
Figure 2 isolates the inference procedure using the same earlier ranking-trained check-
point. Greedy decoding scores 0.712121 Combined and 0.602564 Edge F1; candidate
generation plus likelihood selection scores 0.726368 and 0.615385, gains of 0.014247
and 0.012821 respectively. This jointly tests sampling and selection, not ranking-loss
training. The earlier checkpoint’s greedy score is below pure SFT (0.716113 Combined),
so extra ranking training alone cannot be credited with the inference gain. A matched
extra-SFT control and independent anchor/margin ablations remain unperformed; the
final CORE comparison does not substitute for these controls.

6
Conclusion
We presented a two-stage generate-then-rank approach to goal interpretation for em-
bodied agents: supervised fine-tuning for goal generation, followed by learning to rank
candidate outputs with CORE. On the fixed diagnostic, CORE improves Combined and
Edge F1 over greedy SFT and the implemented DPO comparator, while DPO retains
higher Action F1. The candidate analysis identifies useful goals that generation already
supplies but selection misses.

The evidence is limited to one adaptation seed and a repeatedly inspected 68-item
diagnostic covering five task families. CORE and DPO also differ in pair construction
and optimisation settings, and CORE inference reuses a bank generated by an earlier
ranking-trained checkpoint. These comparisons do not establish an isolated causal effect
of the CORE loss. The anchor and Edge utility weights lack sensitivity ablations, and
serialisation robustness is untested.
The immediate experimental priority is a matched extra-SFT versus CORE contin-
uation from the same parent, holding data, update budget, learning rate, and candidate
bank fixed, followed by anchor/Edge-weight ablations. Multiple training seeds can re-
tain the exact task-disjoint 274/68 split; no diagnostic items need enter training. Such
runs measure optimisation variability, while fresh held-out families are needed to as-
sess generalisation beyond this repeatedly inspected diagnostic. Listwise ranking and
matched constrained decoding remain untested alternatives. Downstream planning suc-
cess and semantic repair require separate evaluation: symbolic F1 alone cannot establish
executability.
