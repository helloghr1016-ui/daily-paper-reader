## Abstract 

Generalist robot policies execute atomic skills reliably but fail when the same skills are composed into long-horizon tasks. Self-improvement from the policy’s own experience does not close this gap: experience collected by the policy alone stays within its own distribution and reproduces what it can already do. We show that what the policy lacks is not a skill but the next step. We present CoachSI, a self-improvement method in which a frozen, off-the-shelf vision-

## 1. Introduction 

Humans become skilled in the physical world not by be-ing shown every motion, but by practice: they act, ob-serve the outcome, and adjust. Repeated over many at-tempts, this loop turns a clumsy first try into a reliable skill, and it continues long after instruction ends. Gener-alist robot policies now perform a wide range of manipula-tion tasks [5, 6, 8, 9, 24, 40–42], yet they rarely improve this way: when a policy fails, making it better typically means collecting new human demonstrations. Enabling a robot to improve from its own experience is the goal of self-improvement in physical AI [7, 35], and it would scale robot learning beyond what human teleoperation can supply. The most direct form of self-improvement changes the policy itself: the robot acts, keeps its successful rollouts, and is fine-tuned on them. This recipe has two limitations. First, a policy acting alone stays within its own distribution, so its successes show it only what it can already do. Sec-ond, on the long-horizon tasks where improvement matters most, the policy fails often and has few successes to collect. Experience improves a policy when it contains successes the policy cannot yet produce, and self-collected experience contains few of them. Existing methods obtain such successes with supervi-sion from outside the policy. Interactive imitation learn-ing brings in a human operator who takes over when the robot errs and supplies corrective actions [2, 23]. Every correction costs operator time, so the supervision scales with human effort, the cost that self-improvement is meant to remove. Reinforcement learning replaces the operator with reward and value models that score the robot’s own at-tempts [10, 45]. These models must themselves be trained before they can supervise the policy. Hierarchical systems place a vision-language model above the policy to decom-pose the task into steps [1, 47]. The policy itself is left un-changed, and the system depends on the high-level model at every deployment. To understand what keeps a policy from producing these successes on its own, we analyze rollouts of generalist poli-cies on long-horizon tasks. We find that the missing suc-cesses are closer than they appear. The policies execute atomic skills reliably, yet fail when the same skills are com-posed into a long-horizon task [16, 29, 37, 48], and the fail-ures take a specific form: partway through the task the pol-icy stalls, and a stalled policy almost never recovers on its own. In a recurring case, the robot is already at the right object and holds the required skill, yet does not act. What the policy lacks in that moment is not a skill but the next step. What it needs is therefore far less than corrective ac-tions or rewards: a single instruction. We introduce CoachSI (Fig. 1), a self-improvement method guided by language alone. Its central component is a language coach: a frozen, off-the-shelf vision-language model (VLM) that watches the robot and replaces the task instruction with the next step, phrased in the format of the policy’s atomic instructions. The coach never outputs an action; every motion is produced by the policy’s own skills. Successful coached rollouts are relabeled in hindsight with the original task instruction [3] and used to fine-tune the policy, which is then deployed on its own. On a World Ac-tion Model, fine-tuning updates only the video backbone and keeps the action backbone frozen. CoachSI needs no human operator, no reward model, and no VLM at test time. We evaluate CoachSI on RoboCasa365 [39] with a World Action Model (WAM) [49] and a Vision-Language-Action model (VLA) [55]. On states where the two poli-cies have stalled, naming the next step raises subgoal com-pletion by 4.6× over the unchanged task instruction, and a VLM coach that sees only the policy’s camera images de-livers 91% of the gain of an oracle that always names the correct step. With the coach, the policy completes more than a quarter of the episodes it fails on its own. After fine-tuning the WAM on coached experience, unassisted success on 18 long-horizon tasks in unseen scenes rises from 47.4% to 59.3%, 9.8 points above fine-tuning on human demon-strations and 7.3 points above fine-tuning on self-collected successes at an equal data budget. With a coach that sees only camera images, it reaches 56.8%, above both base-lines. The action backbone is frozen throughout, so the en-tire gain comes from what the policy predicts will happen next: the skills were already there. Our contributions are: • We identify a class of stalls in long-horizon manipulation in which the policy has the skill but lacks the next step, and show that a single next-step instruction resolves them, for both a WAM and a VLA. • We propose CoachSI, a self-improvement method of lan-guage coaching, hindsight relabeling, and fine-tuning, guided only by a frozen VLM’s next-step instruction. • We show that CoachSI turns coached experience into unassisted capability and outperforms both human demonstrations and self-collected experience at an equal data budget. 

## 2. Related Work 

Self-improving robot policies. A growing body of work improves robot policies with experience collected after pre-2training [35]. Autonomous-improvement systems let the policy practice and train on its own rollouts after hind-sight relabeling or success filtering [7, 64]. Reinforcement-learning methods learn reward or value functions and opti-mize the policy against them [2, 10, 45, 54, 56, 57], with VLMs increasingly serving as the reward model [26, 36]. Interactive imitation learning queries an expert, typically a human teleoperator, for corrective actions on the states the policy visits [2, 23, 31, 34, 44]. Most recently, agents built on LLMs and world models improve the system around the policy: RoboRSI [50] writes and repairs skill code, and RoboCoach [32] selects which subtask demonstrations to collect next. In contrast to these works, CoachSI improves the policy with a single signal, the next-step instruction of a frozen VLM. It trains no reward or value model, requires no action-level expert, and updates the policy itself. 

Language guidance at deployment. Language is the standard interface between high-level reasoning and low-level control. Hierarchical systems decompose a task into language steps at run time, with a separate LLM or VLM planner [1, 18, 20, 21, 28, 47] or inside the pol-icy [4, 5, 58, 60]. A second line corrects the policy in language during execution, with corrections issued by hu-mans [30, 46] or by VLMs that detect and explain fail-ures [15, 33] or choose among the policy’s candidate ac-tion plans [52]. RACER [12] trains both a VLM supervisor and a language-conditioned policy on expert-corrected fail-ure data and keeps the supervisor in the loop at deployment. These systems rely on a human or a high-level model at test time, and several train the policy to follow a new lan-guage interface. CoachSI uses the VLM only during data collection: the coach is frozen and off-the-shelf, it reuses the instruction format of the policy’s atomic skills, and the fine-tuned policy executes the task instruction on its own. 

Language as a training signal. Language also serves as a signal for training. Hindsight relabeling assigns to a trajec-tory the goal it reached [3], and DIAL [53] and SOAR [64] use VLMs to produce such instruction labels for offline or autonomously collected data. BOSS [61] has an LLM pro-pose which skills to chain during practice and learns the chains as new skills. Scaling Up and Distilling Down [17] and Manipulate-Anything [14] have an LLM or a VLM generate demonstrations and distill them into a policy. In these works, language labels experience after the fact, se-lects what the policy practices, or drives a separate data generator. In CoachSI, language intervenes at the point of failure: the coach names the next step to a policy that has stalled, in the format of the policy’s own atomic instruc-tions, so every action in the resulting experience is produced by skills the policy already has. This design lets us mea-sure what language alone contributes, on fixed stalled states of a WAM [49] and a VLA [55] in RoboCasa365 [39] and against the policy’s own successes at an equal data budget. 

World Action Models. A growing family of policies pre-dicts future observations together with actions. UniPi [13] casts control as text-conditioned video generation and ex-tracts actions from the generated video. GR-1 [51] pretrains a transformer on video generation and fine-tunes it to out-put future frames and actions. Video Prediction Policy [19] conditions its action head on the internal representations of a video diffusion model, CoT-VLA [62] predicts a subgoal image before each action chunk, the Unified Video Action model [27] learns a joint video-action latent, and Unified World Models [65] couple video and action diffusion in one model. DreamZero [59] and OpenWAM [49] scale this de-sign into World Action Models (WAMs) built on pretrained video-generation backbones, and Cosmos Policy [25] fine-tunes a pretrained video model directly into a policy. In a WAM, the video backbone predicts what will happen and the action backbone turns the prediction into motor com-mands. CoachSI exploits this separation: coached experi-ence updates only the video backbone, and the action back-bone stays frozen. 

## 3. CoachSI 

3.1. Preliminaries 

We consider a language-conditioned policy πθ [9, 22] that maps an observation ot and an instruction ℓt to an action chunk [11, 63] of length h,

at:t+h−1 ∼ πθ (· | ot, ℓ t). (1) The policy is trained on atomic tasks, each a single skill such as opening a cabinet, and on composite tasks that chain several skills toward one goal. A composite task is specified by a task instruction g. In unassisted execution, the policy is conditioned on ℓt = g for the whole episode. A World Action Model (WAM) implements πθ with two coupled components. A video backbone predicts future frames from the observation and the instruction, and an action backbone predicts the action chunk while attending to the features of the video backbone. The two are de-noised jointly and trained with L = λv Lvideo + λaLaction .A vision-language-action model (VLA) maps observations and language to actions without a video-prediction branch. We fine-tune the WAM, whose separate backbones let us update what the policy predicts next while every action is still produced by the pretrained weights (Sec. 3.3).

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmark and policies. We evaluate on Robo-Casa365 [39], which builds on RoboCasa [38]. The bench-mark provides atomic tasks, each a single skill, and com-posite tasks that chain several of them; we use 18 composite tasks as our long-horizon tasks. We study two public check-points as released: OpenWAM [49], a WAM, and Xiaomi-Robotics-1 [55], a VLA. Data collection and evaluation use disjoint sets of kitchen scenes. 

Stalled states. A rollout under the task instruction stalls when no subgoal changes status for 320 steps. We collect 500 stalled states from failed rollouts of the two policies on 15 tasks. From each state we restore the simulator and con-tinue the same policy under each instruction source, with six continuations per source and matched random seeds. We report subgoal completion , the share of continuations that complete the next subgoal within 320 steps, and task suc-cess within 640 steps. 

Coach. The coach is a frozen Qwen3.8-27B [43]. At every action chunk it receives three camera views and the candi-date next-step instructions of the task, and returns one can-didate or KEEP (Sec. 3.2). The oracle replaces the VLM with a rule that reads subgoal status from the simulator and always selects the first unfinished step; it is the upper bound of a coach. We collect coached data with the image-only coach and with the state-based coach (Sec. 3.2) and report CoachSI for both (Tab. 1); the analyses of Sec. 4.4 use the state-based coach. 

Fine-tuning and evaluation. All fine-tuned policies start from the OpenWAM checkpoint and share one recipe: 3,000 gradient steps at batch size 16, with only the self-attention layers of the video backbone updated (Sec. 3.3). Of the 48,000 training samples, 80% are RoboCasa365 hu-man demonstrations, identical across policies. The remain-ing 20% is the only variable: each of the 18 tasks receives the same budget of 530 samples, filled from human demon-strations, from the policy’s own successes under the task instruction, or from coached successes. We evaluate ev-ery policy under the task instruction alone, on 20 unseen scenes with three trials per task, for 1,080 episodes. Com-parisons are paired by episode, and 95% confidence inter-vals are bootstrapped over tasks. A second training seed confirms the comparison: pooled over both seeds, CoachSI exceeds human demonstrations by 10.0 points and self-collected successes by 5.5. 

4.2. One Next-Step Instruction Resolves the Stall 

Policies have the skills, yet stall. Both policies execute the individual steps reliably: on the atomic tasks of Robo-Casa365, the WAM succeeds in 79.2% of episodes and the VLA in 86.2%. Yet once a long-horizon rollout stalls, it stays stalled: under the task instruction, 97% of continua-tions from a stalled state make no further progress within 320 steps. The failure is therefore not a missing skill. The policy holds a skill it does not invoke. We replace the task instruction with the oracle’s next-step instruction and change nothing else. 4.3. A Frozen VLM Is a Sufficient Coach 

We replace the oracle with the frozen VLM on the 261 stalled states evaluated with every instruction source (Fig. 3b). With camera images as its only input, the VLM coach follows the oracle curve over the full horizon and re-covers 91% of the oracle gain at 320 steps (Fig. 3c); with access to state it recovers all of it. Selecting the next step requires no training, no robot data, and no privileged infor-mation: an off-the-shelf VLM already knows it.

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented CoachSI, a self-improvement method guided by language alone. A frozen VLM coach tells a stalled pol-icy its next step, and fine-tuning on the relabeled successes turns the coached behavior into the policy’s own. A single next-step instruction raises subgoal completion on stalled states by 4.6× on a WAM and a VLA, and an off-the-shelf VLM supplies it from camera images alone. Fine-tuning on the coached experience raises unassisted success in unseen scenes from 47.4% to 59.3%, above both human 8demonstrations and self-collected experience at an equal data budget, with the action backbone frozen. Generalist policies already hold the skills that long-horizon tasks require. What they need is to be told the next step, once.
