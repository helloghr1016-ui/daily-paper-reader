Learning from Physical Experience:
Context-Space Adaptation for Robotic Manipulation
Yihan Zeng1,2,3, Yisu Li1, Kaixin Huang1, Marc WuDunn2, Amelia Kovacs2, Mikael Harseno2,
Kimmy Lin2, Porter Hunley2, Fred Shentu2, Philipp Wu2, Zhongyu Li1, Zike Yan1
1The Chinese University of Hong Kong, 2xdof.ai
3University of Electronic Science and Technology of China
Abstract
Recent multimodal foundation models exhibit strong perception, reasoning, and in-context learning
capabilities, but translating these capabilities into physical action remains challenging. Through
real-world experiments across diverse manipulation tasks, we find that ChatGPT-6-Astra, a
state-of-the-art general-purpose reasoner, can serve as a physically actionable, context-responsive
manipulation policy, demonstrating effective fine manipulation and spatial reasoning. Building
on these findings, we introduce LEXP, a harness for learning from physical experience through
context-space adaptation. LEXP combines task-level planning with an experience module that
interprets execution evidence, extracts and retains lessons with applicability conditions, and reuses
relevant lessons to generate contextual feedback for subsequent actions. Model weights and the
action interface remain fixed throughout. Across 12 real-world manipulation tasks, LEXP achieves
23/36 successful trials (63.9%), compared with 19/36 (52.8%) with memory alone and 2/36 (5.6%)
for vanilla Astra. On three tasks, bread transfer, sort trash and toiletries, and retrieve drawer,
removing planning reduces success from 7/9 to 4/9, while removing retained experience reduces it to
3/9. Execution traces illustrate feedback-guided corrections and cross-episode reuse of an inspection
strategy. Execution analyses illustrate how task structure and experience-derived feedback guide
task progression, action correction, and experience reuse. These results support context-space
adaptation as a practical mechanism for turning physical interaction into reusable guidance for
robotic manipulation.

Introduction
A long-standing goal of robotic manipulation is to perform diverse tasks reliably in unfamiliar and changing
physical environments. This requires robots to adapt when familiar strategies fail and reuse what they learn
from physical interaction. The challenge is to turn limited physical experience into reliable, transferable
improvements in behavior. Existing approaches pursue this goal through different adaptation mechanisms.
Vision–language–action (VLA) models learn from robot demonstrations through supervised fine-tuning
[10, 22]. World action models [29]aim to reduce task-specific adaptation by jointly predicting video and
actions, enabling zero-shot execution of unseen tasks and environments on pretrained embodiments, while
related video-model policies [11] instead use demonstration-based fine-tuning and rollout-informed planning
.

adaptation. General-purpose multimodal reasoners offer a complementary interface: observations, instructions
and execution feedback can condition executable tool calls without updating model weights. Their relevance
to robotics depends on whether this contextual flexibility supports effective action revision and reusable

We investigate this possibility through real-world experiments with GPT-6 Astra [20], across 12 robotic
manipulation tasks covering fine manipulation, spatial reasoning, long-horizon execution, tool use, bimanual
coordination, and force control. Across these tasks, we vary access to interaction history, and examine
recovery demonstrations and online language guidance. Two complementary findings emerge. First, Astra
shows promising fine-manipulation and spatial reasoning capabilities when given access to interaction history:
interaction history inform subsequent corrections and help the robot reason about object relations and pose.
Second, its behavior is steerable through context. Visual demonstrations and textual corrections support
revisions to grasp checking, object repositioning, motion direction, arm selection, and action order. These
benefits vary across tasks, but together highlight both Astra’s executable competence and its capacity for
context steering—two prerequisites for improving manipulation through experience-derived context rather
than additional parameter training.

context-space adaptation (Figure 1). A context compiler combines task instructions, current observations,
interaction history, and agent-generated experience feedback to condition executable actions. A planner

Problem Statement
2.1
Objective: improve policy behavior through reusable experience
Our objective is to improve robotic manipulation by converting physical experience into reusable guidance
for subsequent behavior. Supervised fine-tuning encodes demonstrated actions in model parameters, while
reinforcement learning adjusts a policy using rewards from interaction. We pursue the same practical goal
to improve performance but move the variables being optimized from model parameters to the context that
conditions the policy. With model weights and the robot interface fixed, contextual guidance can change how
the actor advances the current subtask, approaches an object, verifies a grasp, and responds to failure.

fixed robot-control interface. Given a task instruction l and a current observation ot, the actor selects an
action conditioned on a compiled context:

evidence et = (ot, at, rt, ot+1) records the proposed action, its execution result, and the observed physical
consequences.
We implement this adaptation through structured, modular context updates [32]. After each interaction,

2.2
Preliminary capability and context-use evidence of GPT-6 Astra
We investigate the prerequisites for context-space adaptation through real-world experiments with GPT-6
Astra [20] across 12 robotic manipulation tasks covering fine manipulation, spatial reasoning, long-horizon
execution, tool use, bimanual coordination, and force control. We compare execution with and without
interaction history and separately examine the effects of recovery demonstrations and online language feedback.
These experiments examine two empirical prerequisites for context-space adaptation: whether memory enables
the actor to use physical interaction evidence in subsequent decisions, and whether contextual guidance can
steer its behavior.
Memory benefits fine manipulation through outcome-aware corrections. Figure 2a compares Astra’s small-
object pickup performance with and without memory. Overall success rises from 0/9 (0%) without memory
to 8/9 (88.9%) with memory. The task-level contrasts are 0/3 versus 3/3 for cable ties, 0/3 versus 3/3 for
keys, and 0/3 versus 2/3 for the hex key. Here, memory consists of interaction history carried into the actor’s
context, including previous observations, actions and their intention notes, and execution outcomes.
These results show that Astra can execute precision-demanding grasps and that memory guidance can help it
deploy this capability successfully which allows the actor to interpret the current scene in relation to earlier
attempts, assess what an action changed, and use that evidence to revise the next command. Poor performance
4


without memory therefore does not imply that the required manipulation is beyond its execution capability.
Instead, the contrasts motivate studying how prior observations and action outcomes guide subsequent choices.
This benefit is actor-dependent: across the same three tasks, Opus 5.5 improves from 0/9 to 4/9, whereas Sol
5.6 changes from 1/9 to 0/9 (Figure 2b–c). The observations support experience-guided adaptation, while
leaving open which history contents produce the gains.
Memory supports spatial reasoning about object relations and pose. Across two spatial manipulation tasks,
Astra’s observed success increases from 0/3 without memory to 3/3 with memory for grasping an out-of-reach
plush, and from 0/3 to 2/3 for standing a fallen bottle upright. The plush task involves reasoning about
reachability and object relations, while bottle reorientation requires tracking changes in orientation and
support. By retaining previous observations, actions, and execution outcomes, memory makes the object’s
response to manipulation available to subsequent decisions, helping the actor interpret spatial configurations
through interaction rather than appearance alone.

physical interaction into reusable contextual guidance. Memory makes past interactions available, while
demonstrations and language feedback show that context can steer subsequent behavior. The next question is
whether useful guidance can be extracted from the actor’s own successful and failed interactions and reused in
new executions.
3
Method
Building on these observations, we introduce LEXP, a harness for learning from physical experience through
context-space adaptation. As shown in Figure 4 , LEXP combines executable action decisions, task-level
planning, and experience-derived feedback to turn physical interactions into reusable guidance, while keeping
model weights and the action interface fixed. We first present the framework and its information flow in
Section 3.1. We then describe how the actor’s context is constructed and its actions are executed in Section 3.2,
followed by task decomposition and progress tracking in Section 3.3. Finally, Section 3.4 details how physical
experience is extracted, retained, and reused to generate feedback for subsequent actions.
3.1
Overview
Figure 4 illustrates LEXP’s interaction loop. At each decision, a context compiler assembles the system
prompt, task instruction, current visual observations and robot states, memory, and available guidance for the
actor. The actor issues a tool call specifying an action and an accompanying intention note. The execution
layer checks the proposed action before execution and records its outcome. Subsequent observations capture
the resulting robot state and scene, completing an interaction record for later decisions.
Alongside this loop, the planner decomposes the task into subtasks, assesses progress from the observed scene,
and proposes a method for the current subtask. These outputs inform the experience module. The experience
module checks the proposed method against recent interactions and current observations, applies relevant
retained lessons, and generates feedback for the actor’s next action. It also extracts and revises lessons as new
evidence becomes available, preserving their applicability conditions, supporting evidence, and limitations.
Physical interaction thus informs future behavior through both raw history and reusable experience, with the
latter translated into contextual feedback for the current decision.
3.2
Actor Context and Action Execution
The context compiler assembles the actor’s input from the system prompt, task instruction, current camera
observations and robot states, memory and available guidance. Memory contains the proposed action and
its intention note, execution outcome, and observations before and after the action, allowing the actor to
relate the current scene to previous attempts. The planner’s proposed method and retained experience are
interpreted by the experience module and reach the actor through its feedback.
At each decision, the actor produces one tool call containing a motion command and an intention note, or
signals completion or termination. The execution layer validates proposed motions against the latest robot
state and execution constraints, then executes accepted commands. The command, execution outcome, and

3.3
Task-Level Planning
Planning provides task-level structure for reasoning and execution. Individual action decisions must serve the
overall task, while retained lessons must be interpreted in relation to what the robot is currently trying to
accomplish. The planner supplies this structure by decomposing the task into an ordered list of subtasks. As
execution proceeds, it assesses progress against the observed scene, identifies the current subtask, and proposes
a method for carrying it out. Together, the current subtask and proposed method give the experience module
a reference for assessing recent execution and applying relevant lessons to the current situation. Through the
resulting feedback, planning guides task-level reasoning and the application of experience.
3.4
From Physical Experience to Context Space Adaptation
The experience module turns physical interaction into contextual guidance by combining recent execution
evidence with retained lessons. It receives the current subtask and proposed method from the planner, recent
interaction records, and current observations. It first checks whether the proposed method’s condition is
satisfied and compares observed execution with the method. This assessment grounds feedback in what the
robot commanded and what the observations show, rather than assuming that an accepted command achieved
its intended physical effect.
Extracting and retaining experience.
Alongside feedback generation, the module records useful findings
from completed interactions. These may include observed object relations, attempted methods and their
results, partial successes, unsuccessful attempts, and execution constraints. Each lesson is retained with its
applicability conditions, its potential use in later decisions, supporting interaction records, and limitations or
unresolved explanations. New evidence can revise an existing lesson, allowing retained experience to change as
the robot encounters additional physical evidence. Unlike raw history, these entries preserve selected findings
after the original interactions leave the actor’s history window.
7


Reusing experience through contextual feedback.
For subsequent decisions, the module considers lessons
retained from the current attempt or carried over from earlier attempts. It evaluates their applicability
against the current observations, using the planner’s subtask and proposed method as a task-level reference.
Relevant lessons inform a concise feedback message supplied to the actor before its next action; when retained
experience conflicts with current observations, the observations take precedence. Experience reuse therefore
involves interpreting prior findings in the current physical situation and translating them into actionable
contextual guidance.
4
Experiments
We evaluate LEXP through real-world manipulation experiments organized around four questions: Q1, Does
LEXP improve performance on real world manipulation tasks? Q2, How does task-level planning guide
the actor’s reasoning and task progression? Q3, Can lessons extracted from physical interaction guide later
decisions beyond the interactions from which they were learned? Q4, How does experience-derived feedback
shape the actor’s action decisions?
4.1
Experimental Setup
Platform and tasks.
We conduct experiments on a bimanual YAM robot equipped with two grippers and
three view realsense cameras. The actor receives camera observations and robot states and controls the robot
through a tool-call interface. Our evaluation covers 12 manipulation tasks spanning fine manipulation, spatial
reasoning, long-horizon execution, tool use, bimanual coordination, and force control. Figure 5 illustrates
the 12 tasks and representative stages of their execution. Task details are provided in Appendix A. The
preliminary experiments in Section 2.2 characterize memory and context steering; here, we evaluate LEXP’s
manipulation performance and examine the roles of task-level planning and experience reuse.
Comparison conditions.
We compare the same actor without memory, with memory, and with LEXP. The
memory condition retains previous observations, actions and intention notes, and execution outcomes. LEXP
additionally uses task-level planning and experience-derived feedback as described in Sections 3.3 and 3.4.
Model weights and the action interface remain fixed across conditions.
Protocol and metrics.
For each task, we conduct three trials. Trials across comparison conditions use similar
distributions of initial configurations. Retained experience is initialized according to the evaluated condition,
with any experience carried over from earlier attempts explicitly identified. Each trial is limited to 100 actor
decisions and ends when the actor signals completion, or when the budget is exhausted. Task success is
assessed by human annotations using predefined physical completion criteria, independently of the actor’s
completion declaration. We report success as the number of successful trials out of three and subtask progress
as the total number of completed annotated subtasks divided by the total number of evaluated subtasks
across trials, using annotations independent of the planner’s generated checklist. Task-specific initialization
procedures and success criteria are provided in Appendix A.
4.2
Q1: Does LEXP improve performance on real world manipulation tasks?
To answer Q1, we compare vanilla Astra, Astra with memory, and Astra equipped with LEXP across 12
real-world manipulation tasks. As shown in Figure 6a, LEXP achieves 23/36 successful trials (63.9%),
compared with 19/36 (52.8%) with memory and 2/36 (5.6%) without memory. Retaining interaction history
substantially improves observed performance, while LEXP provides a further aggregate gain of 11.1 percentage
points over memory alone. Complete task-level results, including the other evaluated models, are reported in
Appendix D, Table 2.
Figure 6c shows that the additional benefit over memory is concentrated in long-horizon execution, where
success increases from 3/12 to 7/12. Tool-use and force-control tasks also show higher aggregate success, while
fine-manipulation and spatial-reasoning performance remains unchanged in aggregate. Bimanual coordination
remains challenging, with no successful sweeping or towel-folding trials under any condition. The following
analyses examine how planning, feedback, and retained experience influence execution.

4.3
Q2: How does task-level planning guide the actor’s reasoning and task progression?
Planning provides task-level structure for reasoning and execution. To examine its contribution, we remove
the planner while retaining memory, the experience module, and experience-derived feedback. Across bread
transfer, drawer retrieval, and item sorting, complete LEXP achieves 7/9 successful trials and 24/27 completed
subtasks, compared with 4/9 and 16/27 without the planner (Figure 7).
The actor can already reason about how to complete these tasks, but execution requires maintaining task
objectives through repeated actions and recovery. Different tasks impose different demands, bread transfer
follows a relatively direct acquisition then transfer then placement sequence, whereas sorting requires preserving
item and container assignments and drawer search requires tracking inspected drawers and opening constraints.
Item sorting illustrates the distinction between initial task reasoning and its sustained use. In the memory-only
example, the actor initially assigns the bottle to the trash bin and later releases the paper into the pink
basket. Without the planner, experience-derived feedback already provides task-level guidance: before the
first action, it asks the actor to establish the sorting rule rather than infer destinations from proximity. The
actor subsequently states the correct item and container assignments in its action note. Later, feedback
recognizes that the paper is inside the bin and redirects attention to the remaining bottle, demonstrating
implicit progress tracking. Although the actor revisits the container choice during the prolonged execution
and again selects the basket, bottle placement remains incomplete.

4.4

the interactions from which they were learned?
Physical experience becomes reusable guidance in context space. To examine the contribution of retained

not retain or access stored lessons. Across the three evaluated tasks, complete LEXP achieves 7/9 successful
trials and 24/27 completed subtasks, compared with 3/9 and 12/27 without retained experience (Figure 7).
Observed task success is higher with retained experience on all three tasks. This comparison supports the
utility of explicit lesson retention and reuse in the evaluated settings.

In Episode 1, the actor recognizes that the bread lies between the tong shafts and retracts and reorients the
tool to improve tip visibility in the top-camera view. The selected inspection action begins approximately
333 s after the first actor call. Retained experience records when this strategy applies and the need to verify
tip alignment and bread co-motion. In Episodes 2 and 3, the actor executes a free-wrist camera repositioning
action approximately 88 s and 83 s after the first actor call, respectively. The retained lesson is subsequently
refined to include free-wrist inspection when tool-hand adjustments do not resolve visibility. These traces
illustrate inspection behavior and experience reused across episodes.

5
Related work
5.1
Foundation Models for Robotic Manipulation
Foundation models support robotic manipulation through different interfaces between perception, reasoning,
and action. Robot-specialized models such as OpenVLA [10] and π0.5 [22] learn action generation from
robot data, while DreamZero [29] and Cosmos Policy [11] connect visual prediction with action generation.
These approaches encode manipulation capabilities through model training. A complementary direction
uses general-purpose language and multimodal models to reason over tasks and invoke an external action
interface. Here, the central challenge is to connect semantic reasoning with physically feasible execution.
Different interfaces address this challenge at different levels. SayCan [2] grounds language-based task planning
in the affordances of available skills, while Code as Policies [13] expresses reasoning through executable
programs that call perception and control APIs. VoxPoser [8] translates language and visual observations
into spatial value maps for motion generation, and ReKep [9] represents manipulation objectives through
relational keypoint constraints. Together, these methods show how pretrained reasoning can direct physical
behavior through skills, programs, and geometric representations. They also highlight the importance of the
surrounding execution system: a model’s reasoning must be translated into actions whose consequences can
be observed and checked. Recent work increasingly examines this interaction between model capability and
12


execution scaffolding. CaPX [6] studies code-based manipulation with structured execution feedback and skill
synthesis, while concurrent evaluations investigate general-purpose multimodal reasoners as robot policies
[4, 19, 26, 33]. Our preliminary evaluation follows this direction by examining fine manipulation, spatial
reasoning, and responsiveness to context across real-world tasks. Building on these capabilities, LEXP studies
how evidence from physical execution can guide subsequent decisions while keeping both model parameters
and the action interface fixed.
5.2
In-context Robot Learning
In-context robot learning allows behavior to depend on examples supplied at deployment. ICRT [5] conditions
action prediction on sensorimotor trajectories, enabling demonstrations to specify new behaviors without
task-specific parameter updates. RICL [25] develops in-context learning capabilities in a pretrained robot
policy and retrieves relevant demonstration segments at deployment. RoboPrompt [30] instead represents
demonstrations through textual descriptions of object poses and end-effector actions for an off-the-shelf
language model. These approaches demonstrate that contextual examples can communicate task-relevant
behavior, although they differ in the representation of demonstrations and the training required to make those
examples usable. Feedback extends context conditioning beyond examples supplied before execution. Inner
Monologue [7] incorporates scene descriptions, success feedback, and human input into closed-loop planning,
while InCoRo [36] investigates in-context control with interaction feedback. Language corrections can also
communicate changes that are difficult to specify through a scalar success signal: interactive corrections
[23] and Language Model Predictive Control [14] use such information to revise robot behavior. These
methods establish context as an interface for changing plans and actions during execution. Our preliminary
demonstration and language-feedback experiments examine this interface in the evaluated manipulation
setting. A further question is how corrective information is obtained and retained. REFLECT [15] uses
multisensory execution summaries to explain failures and support corrective planning. DROC [31] distills
human corrections into reusable knowledge and retrieves relevant corrections for subsequent tasks and object
instances. These approaches connect immediate feedback with more persistent behavioral guidance. LEXP
develops this connection using the robot’s own physical interactions: raw history records what happened, while
an experience module extracts lessons, retains their applicability conditions and limitations, and interprets
them in relation to the current subtask. The resulting feedback brings relevant experience into the actor’s
context.
5.3
Learning from Experience and Context Adaptation
Physical interaction can improve robot behavior through updates to policies, skills, or external knowledge.
Reinforcement-learning systems such as SERL [17] and HIL-SERL [18] improve policies using real-world
interaction, with human intervention supporting data collection and recovery. π0.6 [21] likewise studies
improvement from experience for generalist robot policies. These approaches update model parameters to
incorporate interaction evidence. LEXP pursues improvement through a different adaptation variable: the
contextual guidance supplied to a frozen actor. Experience can also modify the structures surrounding a
policy. ReSYNC [12] connects failure recovery with the acquisition of relational concepts for abstract planning.
ASPIRE [16] refines reusable robot skills through execution evidence, while ENPIRE [28] organizes physical
policy improvement through an agentic experimentation loop. More recent systems extend adaptation to the
execution harness itself. SHAPER [27] evolves reusable skills and a context-code harness with frozen model
parameters, and Harness VLA [34] uses execution memory to guide the deployment of fixed manipulation
primitives. These works show that experience-driven improvement can operate at several levels beyond policy
weights, including programs, abstractions, skills, and contextual knowledge. Related developments in language
agents provide mechanisms for retaining and applying experience in textual form. Reflexion [24] uses verbal
reflections as memory for subsequent attempts, and ExpeL [35] extracts reusable insights from accumulated
experiences. GEPA [1] uses execution traces and reflection to optimize prompts, while Training-Free GRPO
[3] distills experiential knowledge into contextual guidance. ACE [32] further organizes adaptation through the
incremental generation, reflection, and curation of contextual knowledge. These methods motivate treating
context as a persistent adaptation mechanism rather than only a record of the current interaction. Applying
this principle to manipulation requires grounding retained guidance in physical evidence. An intended action
may not execute as proposed, an executed motion may not achieve its intended effect, and a previously
13


useful correction may depend on object pose, contact state, or task progress. LEXP therefore connects
task-level planning with evidence-based experience extraction and reuse. The planner supplies the current
task structure; the experience module interprets recent interactions and retained lessons against the observed
scene, then generates feedback for the actor. Our evaluation examines how this organization affects real-world
manipulation performance, task progression, and the reuse of interaction-derived guidance.
6
Conclusion
We propose LEXP, a harness for learning from physical experience through context-space adaptation. LEXP
combines task-level planning with the extraction and reuse of interaction-derived lessons, translating physical
evidence into contextual feedback without updating model weights. Across 12 real-world manipulation tasks,
LEXP achieves 23/36 successful trials (63.9%), compared with 19/36 (52.8%) with memory alone and 2/36
(5.6%) for vanilla Astra. On three tasks, removing planning reduces success from 7/9 to 4/9, while removing
retained experience reduces it to 3/9. Execution analyses illustrate how feedback guides action correction and
how retained experience informs a later episode. These results highlight the potential of physical experience
as reusable guidance for robotic manipulation.

Preliminary Context-Steering Setup
The preliminary experiments examine whether externally supplied context can redirect execution and recovery.
These inputs are distinct from the agent-generated experience feedback evaluated in LEXP.
B.1
Visual Demonstrations
A visual demonstration is a human-recorded video showing how a task is performed. It provides an execution
reference, including where present, recovery from unsuccessful interactions. We process each recording into
candidate frames, from which the model selects frames relevant to the task. The selected frames are inserted
into the actor’s context as visual references, alongside observations of the robot’s current scene. They condition
the actor’s decisions without directly specifying robot motion commands.
Specifically, frame selection is performed through a separate, stateless model request containing the task
instruction and timestamped candidate images. The model selects a fixed number of frames, including the
first and last frames, and provides a relevance explanation for each selection. The frame-selection prompt and
the actor’s demonstration-input format are shown below.
Demonstration frame-selection system prompt
You are preparing to perform a robot manipulation task. Select demonstration frames most useful for completing
the task with the robot: task stages, object relationships and before/after effects. Return frame IDs and a short
explanation of each frame’s relevance. No robot movement is allowed in this phase.
Frame-selection input template
TASK: <task instruction>
DEMONSTRATION TYPE: <demonstration type>
Select exactly <number of selected frames> frames, including endpoints <first frame ID> and <last frame ID>.
frame <candidate frame ID>, t=<timestamp>s
Camera: video
<candidate image; repeated for each candidate>
After selected frames, for human demonstrations, the actor receives the following context block.
Visual demonstration in the actor context

B.2
Online Language Feedback
Online language feedback consists of human-provided textual guidance during robot execution. The feedback
is inserted into the actor’s context before a subsequent decision, allowing the actor to interpret it together
with current observations and generate the corresponding tool call.
Pending messages are inserted into the actor’s context using the following wrapper:
Online language feedback in the actor context
OPERATOR FEEDBACK:
<human message>
<additional pending messages, if any>
The actor interprets the message together with its current observations and produces its own tool call.
The barbecue-transfer analysis in Section 2.2 relates delivered messages to subsequent changes in motion
direction, clearance, arm selection, and action order.
C
LEXP Implementation Details
C.1
Experience Representation and Updates
An experience entry contains:
• lesson: what was attempted or observed and what followed;
• applicability: when the finding applies and what would invalidate it;
• feedback_use: which later decision the finding can inform;
• limits_and_counterevidence: what the evidence does not establish;
• unresolved: alternative explanations or simultaneous changes that remain unresolved;
• evidence_ids: supporting interaction references;
• revises: the prior entry replaced by an update, where applicable.
The applicability of a retained lesson is distinct from the condition attached to a proposed method. The
former concerns reuse of a past finding; the latter concerns whether a current proposal is appropriate.
Experience updates are generated alongside contextual feedback. Each update either creates a new entry
or identifies an existing entry through revises. A revision supersedes the referenced entry in the active
experience list while preserving its provenance. The resulting list is supplied to subsequent feedback calls.
When cross-episode seeding is enabled, findings from earlier attempts are selected and compressed to initialize
the new episode’s list.

E.1
Actor
The actor system prompt is assembled from embodiment and coordinate conventions, the action contract,

You control a physical robot through one tool call per decision. Observations are measured inputs; action

Gripper opening is normalized: 0 closed, 1 open. Camera order: <camera_roles>.
<factual_action_contract>

<station_geometry>

Verify instruction
After execution, compare the new observation with the intended effect. An accepted command does not establish
task success.
21


Retry instruction
On a failed attempt, use observed evidence to change the next attempt. Avoid repeating an unchanged action
after the same rejection.
Terminate instruction
Call done only when observable task conditions are satisfied. Call give_up only when the task is truly impossible
in the current episode: concrete observation or guard feedback establishes that the remaining requirement cannot
be completed safely, or multiple materially different, evidence-driven attempts have failed in the same way and
no untried safe, feasible recovery strategy remains. A single missed grasp, failed placement, IK refusal, poor view,
apparent reach boundary, uncertainty, or elapsed effort is not sufficient. While any safe, feasible strategy remains
within the call budget, continue with a meaningfully changed attempt, such as repositioning, changing viewpoint,
orientation, approach, arm, or reachable grasp point.
Factual Cartesian contract
EMBODIMENT: YAM arms [’left’, ’right’], with parallel-jaw grippers. CONTROL: absolute Cartesian poses.
Each arm has its own base frame: +x forward, +y left, +z up. There is no shared world frame. Positions are
metres; angles are radians. Movement tools do not change the gripper; set_gripper holds arm position. Gripper:
0 closed, 1 open. Omitted dimensions keep their measured current value (zero offset in delta mode). One tool
call is executed before the next decision observation. Orientation roll/pitch/yaw are relative to ’down’. All zero
is the reference orientation. <measured_arm_proportions>

E.2
Planner
Planning uses an initial decomposition call followed by joint progress-assessment and method-proposal calls.
The initial decomposition separates task goals from candidate methods. Subsequent calls receive the existing
checklist, recent attempted actions, the task, and current scene observations.
Initial decomposition prompt
Break one physical robot manipulation task into an ordered list of subtasks.
Each subtask is a single physical change another observer could watch happen and could afterwards confirm from
a photograph of the scene. Together they must cover the task and nothing beyond it. At most 12 subtasks.
Do not say how any of them is to be carried out. That is asked for separately, one step at a time, by a later call
that can see what has already been tried.
Joint progress and method prompt
Say where a robot has got to in a fixed checklist, and what it should do now.
Read it off the scene alone: what these images show has happened to the objects, not the robot’s account of
what it did. Put the id of the step to work on now in next_step_id, and in next_step_why say what in these
22


images makes it the one. When the images show nothing is left for the robot to do, leave next_step_id empty
and say what shows the task is over. A step whose effect the scene was never going to keep is not work that
remains: once what it led to is there, it is behind the robot.
Everything before that step counts as passed, everything from it on as still to do, and you may not reword a step
that is already there. If the scene has gone backwards – something dropped, something undone – name the step
that has to be done again; that is how going backwards is said here.
When the step in play moves forward, say in passed_evidence what these images show about the one just passed.
Leave it empty when the step in play has not changed, and empty when it has gone back: that reading is made
once, where it happened, and is not written again afterwards.
The checklist itself may gain or lose a step, and only in those two ways. ADD one when this scene shows
something no step accounts for – say in why what you are looking at, not that a step seems missing. RETIRE
one when the scene shows it is not needed after all; it stays on the list, marked, because that it was planned and
found unnecessary is worth knowing. You may not retire a step the run has already passed. Both lists empty is
the ordinary answer.
Return two separate outputs. First judge the checklist under plan. Then propose current_method for the step
you name in next_step_id. The method goes to an advice module, not directly to the robot. A proposed way of
acting is not an extra goal or evidence of success; never add a subtask merely to require your method. Keep
goals and means distinct. Describe what contacts, supports or moves relative to what, without coordinates,
joint angles or gripper commands. State its applicability condition (use ’none’ if none) and expected observable
change; neither is a claim that it happened. current_method addresses the step you name in next_step_id;
nothing rewrites that. New subtask IDs continue after the highest existing s-number, in addition-list order. When
next_step_id is empty, all four current_method fields must be empty. Otherwise text must be nonempty and at
most 400 characters; condition and expected_observable_change each at most 400 characters.
E.3
Experience Module
The feedback call receives the task, current subtask, proposed method, robot or station reference facts,
recent interaction records, current observations, and available retained lessons. Its structured assessment and
feedback are separate: only the text field is delivered to the actor, while experience updates are retained and
applied records document lesson use.
Feedback and extraction prompt
You are watching a robot carry out a task, with its own recorded reasoning, what it commanded, what the
station measured change, and the current cameras.
A method it is working to may be given below, and it may name a condition it depends on. First quote that
condition and say whether these images show it satisfied, then settle how what the records and images show
actually happened stands against the method. Then write the one line the robot is given before its next move.
Base both on what these records and images show, not on what the task is supposed to look like. The line reaches
the robot once and is then gone.
You may also record what this run has found out, for decisions it will make once these interactions have scrolled
out of view. Look over the completed interactions before deciding what to keep, and consider all of these without
needing one of each: a scene observation or relationship that will still matter once its raw evidence is gone; a
method that was tried and what was observed to come of it, including exploration the robot started itself, a local
or partial success, a boundary where something was refused, and an outcome left unsettled; a change to when
something already recorded still applies.
A first useful discovery can be experience: it need not contradict a previous belief, be novel world knowledge, or
achieve the whole task. Do not withhold one merely because its source is still in the interactions above; that they
will expire is the reason for writing it down. Give a method the robot proposed itself no less weight than one
that came from advice. Keeping nothing is right when nothing was found out, and wrong when something was.
Retained-experience input instruction
WHAT THIS ROBOT HAS FOUND OUT ON THIS TASK, worked out earlier in this run or carried in from
earlier attempts; each line says which. Readings, not human instructions, verified causal rules, or current scene
facts: the interactions they came from may no longer be shown above, which is why they are written down at all.

Cross-episode initialization.
When experience seeding is enabled, a separate call selects and compresses
findings from earlier attempts into the lesson list used to initialize the new episode. This call reads retained
findings, rather than the complete source trajectories.
Experience-seeding prompt
Below is what earlier attempts at this task on this station found out: for each, what was observed, when it
applies, which decision it can constrain, what it does not establish, and anything it could not tell apart. Write
what this episode should start out already knowing. It faces a different arrangement of the same task on the same
station, so carry only what will still be true there – an applicability that names this one arrangement cannot
be used in another. Keep what each entry does not establish; dropping it is how a local result becomes a rule.
Everything you carry costs this episode something to read, and carrying nothing is a legitimate answer.
