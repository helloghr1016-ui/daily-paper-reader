## Abstract 

Agentic harnesses enable frontier vision-language models (VLMs) to manipulate robots through perception, plan-ning, and control tools, yet achieving comparable perfor-mance with compact open-weight VLMs remains challeng-ing. We present RoboHarness-RL , an agentic reinforce-ment learning framework for improving VLM-based robot manipulation through tool calls. Starting from supervised fine-tuning on successful teacher demonstrations, the VLM further improves through multi-turn reinforcement learn-ing with task-outcome rewards, without relying on vision-language-action models or world action models. To sup-port efficient multi-turn training, we develop LiteHarness ,which uses hierarchical memory to keep interaction con-texts compact while preserving task performance. With Qwen3.5-9B, RL raises success from 31.2% to 44.0% on RoboTwin 2.0 and from 57.8% to 73.3% on LIBERO-Pro; these gains extend to randomized RoboTwin scenes unseen during training (26.4% to 38.0%). ## 1. Introduction 

Agentic harnesses let vision-language models (VLMs) ma-nipulate robots through tool calls for perception, planning, and control [6, 28, 61]. Robot systems have traditionally separated a slow model that reasons about the task (Sys-tem 2) from a fast learned policy that produces motor ac-tions (System 1). In a harness, this boundary blurs: the VLM not only decides what to do next, but also specifies end-effector poses and gripper commands, so its decisions directly shape how the robot moves. Yet achieving comparable performance with compact open-weight VLMs remains challenging. On randomized RoboTwin 2.0 scenes, the ORA harness reaches 69.2% suc-cess with DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash but only 6.8% with the open-weight Qwen3.5-9B (Sec. 4.3). Building on these advances, we in-vestigate whether reinforcement learning from the model’s own interactions can further improve manipulation perfor-mance beyond supervised imitation. Reinforcement learning (RL) has improved vision-language-action models (VLAs) [5, 27, 49] and world ac-tion models (WAMs) [41, 62] beyond imitation. In paral-lel, agentic RL trains language models through multi-turn tool use in digital environments such as web search [22] and graphical interfaces [50]. Harness-based manipulation lies between the two (Fig. 2): the VLM uses high-level tool calls as in agentic RL, while controlling physical robot ac-tions. Concurrent approaches include RL with a learned VLA executor [35] and single-turn RL for control-program generation [10]. We instead train the VLM to specify robot actions itself through multi-turn tool calls. Two challenges make RL costly in this setting. First, every model call carries a growing interaction history, in-cluding camera images, across episodes of up to 200 calls, increasing the cost of both rollout collection and policy up-dates. Second, tasks that the current policy consistently solves or fails may provide little group-relative learning sig-nal, yet still consume rollout budget. We address both with RoboHarness-RL , an agentic RL framework that trains VLMs to manipulate through tool calls, with no VLA or WAM generating actions. After fine-tuning on teacher demonstrations, the VLM learns through multi-turn GRPO with task-outcome rewards. Interac-tion runs through LiteHarness , whose hierarchical mem-ory keeps recent interactions in detail, summarizes older ac-tions, and records failures separately. Compared with com-pressed full history, it roughly halves the prompt tokens per call while achieving higher success. Adaptive scheduling postpones solved tasks, removes persistently failed ones, and allocates more rollouts to tasks with a learning signal. With Qwen3.5-9B [42], RL raises success from 31.2% after SFT to 44.0% on RoboTwin 2.0 [4] and from 57.8% to 73.3% on LIBERO-Pro. Although trained only in clean scenes, the policy also improves from 26.4% to 38.0% in randomized RoboTwin scenes. RL also succeeds on tasks that SFT fails to solve in evaluation. Behavioral analysis shows how RL changes the way the model uses the harness: on RoboTwin, repetitive-call rates drop from about 10% to under 3%, jointly successful episodes require about a third fewer model calls, and the policy more often recovers to complete the task after execution failures (Fig. 1c). Our main contributions are as follows: • We propose RoboHarness-RL , an agentic RL framework that trains VLMs to manipulate robots through multi-turn tool calls without relying on VLAs or WAMs. • We design LiteHarness and adaptive task scheduling for efficient multi-turn RL. Hierarchical memory roughly halves prompt tokens per call and improves success. • We show that RL improves over supervised imitation by 

12.8 and 15.5 percentage points on RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO-Pro, respectively. These gains extend to ran-domized scenes unseen during training. RL also reduces repetitive tool calls, shortens successful executions, and improves recovery after negative feedback. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Agentic harnesses for robot manipulation 

Early language-guided systems compose predefined skills [1, 16, 46], generate policy code [29], or guide motion planning with spatial representations [17]; hierar-chical VLM planning also supports long-horizon mobile manipulation [52]. Recent harnesses enable closed-loop control through code and tools. Code-based systems compose perception and control primitives and revise programs from execu-tion feedback [10, 11, 21, 39]. Tool-based systems expose perception, planning, and control functions for direct in-vocation [6, 28, 61, 64, 67, 68]. Other harnesses connect VLM motion commands to deterministic control with asyn-chronous monitoring [23]. Across episodes, agents refine code through repeated trials [57, 59], evolve harnesses around frozen mod-els [12, 53], or retain experience as persistent mem-ory [36, 51], transferable spatial lessons [65], and reusable skills [15]. RoboHarness-RL instead improves model deci-sions through multi-turn RL. 

2.2. Agentic reinforcement learning for language models 

Agentic RL trains language models through multi-turn in-teraction, including search [22, 71], tool-assisted reason-ing [8, 26], and graphical or web interfaces [50, 56]. 2.3. Training embodied agents 

Robot policies use sparse plans with action experts [32], sparse trajectory interfaces [18], or joint-aligned actions and adversarial learning for cross-embodiment demonstra-tion transfer [70]. Yet demonstration learning can leave modality shortcuts [34] and insufficient recovery-state cov-erage [33]. RL improves VLAs and diffusion policies be-yond imitation [5, 19, 24, 27, 37, 49, 54], and extends to world action models [41, 62]. SFT and multi-turn RL are also studied for embodied agents acting on visual feed-back [25]. For robot harnesses, teacher distillation transfers frontier-model capabilities to compact agents [6, 12, 28]. CaP-RL studies single-turn RL for control programs, while Guava explores RL refinement on two selected manipula-tion tasks [10, 31]. AgenticRoboRL [35] invokes VLAs through RPent and generates code with privileged state through CaP-X; PyRUA-Lean Coder [45] generates Python programs over primitives including frozen VLAs. CaPtain combines SFT and multi-turn RL for code-as-policy agents with a privileged-state critic [7], while ZAP uses manu-ally designed progress rewards [40]. We use direct tool calls, task-success rewards, and general tool-use penalties, without VLAs, privileged-state critics, or task-specific re-ward shaping. We train and evaluate on RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO-Pro with hierarchical memory and adaptive task scheduling. 

## 3. Method 

3.1. Overview 

RoboHarness-RL trains a VLM policy through multi-turn interaction with a robot environment. Interaction is sup-ported by LiteHarness , a lightweight harness built on the tools of ORA [61] and designed for efficient agentic RL. Figure 3 illustrates the harness and the two training stages. For a manipulation task with instruction x, a VLM pol-icy πθ , parameterized by θ, interacts with the environment through LiteHarness. At model call t, the policy generates a response yt ∼ πθ (· | x, h t), where ht denotes the inter-action context provided by LiteHarness for that call. Tool calls within these responses provide access to perception, 3planning, and control, with execution feedback guiding sub-sequent decisions. The resulting interaction is represented by a trajectory τ = (( h1, y 1), . . . , (hT , y T )) , where T is the number of model calls until the episode ends. Training proceeds in two stages: supervised fine-tuning on teacher demonstrations establishes basic manipulation procedures, followed by multi-turn agentic RL to improve decision-making through interaction. Both stages optimize only the VLM policy, keeping the perception models and motion planners fixed. Section 3.2 describes LiteHarness, Section 3.3 the two training stages, and Section 3.4 interac-tion budgets and task scheduling. 

3.2. LiteHarness: A Lightweight Agent Harness 

LiteHarness is built around one constraint: every model call carries the interaction history, so the length of this context drives the cost of both rollout collection and policy opti-mization. It keeps the context compact in two ways, through concise tool descriptions and through hierarchical memory. 

Tool interface. The policy acts on the environment only through tools, which fall into two kinds. Read-only queries return information without moving the robot, such as object masks from SAM 3 [3], grasp candidates from Contact-GraspNet [48], or geometric measurements. Robot-action tools end the policy’s turn and execute grasp selections, end-effector targets, or gripper commands specified by the VLM. Motion execution uses cuRobo [47] planning or lo-cal control, while gripper commands control opening and closing. Concise descriptions of each tool’s purpose and arguments keep the fixed context short. Full interface spec-ifications are provided in Appendix B.1. 

Hierarchical memory. Hierarchical memory controls context growth by retaining different parts of the interac-tion history at different levels of detail. Without it, feed-back from every query and action would accumulate, and the policy often issues several queries before each robot ac-tion. Omitting fixed instructions, the context is written as 

ht = [ St; Ft; Rt], (1) where St, Ft, and Rt denote historical summaries, failure records, and recent interactions, respectively. Here, [ · ; · ]

denotes concatenation, and all memory components contain only information available before model call t.

St summarizes actions older than the recent window and their outcomes to maintain a concise account of task progress. To discourage repeated unsuccessful attempts, Ft

consolidates failed actions and rejected calls with their key arguments and reported causes, keeping prior failures acces-sible after their details leave the window. Recent memory 

Rt retains the current observation and up to K preceding observations, together with their associated responses and tool feedback. Earlier queries within this window are com-pressed while preserving key outputs, such as coordinates and candidate identifiers, for subsequent tool use. Images use a shorter window: only the current and two preceding steps can contribute images. Tool-feedback im-ages from previous observations are discarded, and each re-quest is capped at Bimg images, removing the oldest first. All memory operations are deterministic and require no ad-ditional model calls; retention and compression settings are provided in Appendix B.4.

The teacher acts under the same mem-ory rules, and each of its responses is paired with the con-text the student would receive at that call. 3.4. Interaction Budgets and Task Scheduling 

Interaction budgets. Each episode is limited by a model-call budget Bcall , which counts every model call including read-only queries, and a robot-action budget Bact . For each task, Bcall is chosen from 80, 120, 160, and 200 calls so that it covers the longest successful teacher and SFT episodes. 

Bact is 80, and an episode ends when either budget is ex-hausted or a terminal condition is reached. 

Adaptive task scheduling. To improve training effi-ciency, we reduce sampling of tasks that consistently suc-ceed or fail and allocate more rollouts to the remaining tasks. Based on recent results, RoboHarness-RL applies three rules. (1) A task whose latest group fully suc-ceeds skips one sampling cycle and is revisited before an-other skip, reducing sampling while maintaining coverage. (2) Tasks whose rollouts fail in two sampled groups are re-moved from the pool at a stage boundary. (3) On LIBERO-Pro, where SFT already solves most episodes of the Spatial and Object suites, later rounds draw twice as many groups from the harder Goal and Long suites. Budget values and scheduling parameters are provided in Appendix D.4. 

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmark and protocol. We train a separate policy on each of RoboTwin 2.0 [4] and LIBERO-Pro [72], with dis-joint training and evaluation initial states. RoboTwin has 50 bimanual tasks; we train only in clean scenes and eval-uate on five held-out clean states per task with unseen in-structions and on ORA’s randomized set [61], which adds distractors and varies backgrounds, lighting, and tabletop height (250 episodes each). LIBERO-Pro covers the Spa-tial, Object, Goal, and Long suites under task and swap perturbations, with ten tasks per variant and ten initial states each (800 episodes). Success follows the official task checks, and SFT and RL are evaluated on the same instances and settings. 

Implementation details. Both policies use Qwen3.5-9B [42] and LiteHarness. SFT uses 393 and 781 successful GPT-6 Astra demonstrations for RoboTwin and LIBERO-Pro, respectively. We train for two epochs and initialize RL from the resulting SFT checkpoints. Both stages use rank-16 LoRA with α = 32 and a frozen vision encoder, while perception tools and motion planners remain fixed. Think-ing is disabled throughout training and evaluation to keep multi-turn interaction affordable. Each RL rollout batch samples eight trajectories for each of twelve task–initial-state pairs at temperature 1.0. We report checkpoints after 25 sampling rounds on both benchmarks. LiteHarness re-tains the current observation and up to three preceding ob-servations in recent memory, with at most 24 images per model call. Model-call budgets are set to 80–200 depend-ing on the task, with a separate limit of 80 robot decisions per episode. Training and evaluation use the same limits. Evaluation uses temperature 0.7, top-p 0.8, and top-k 20. All training runs on eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Full train-ing configurations are provided in Appendix D.

4.4. Behavioral Analysis 

RL reduces repetitive tool use. We count consecutive calls with identical arguments as repetitive when they cannot yield a new result: queries repeated without a state change, or actions retried after rejection or failure to move the robot. Repeated actions that move the robot, such as successive pushes, are excluded. Figure 5a illustrates such repetition: SFT retries the same invalid compute call until its budget is exhausted. RL reduces the repetition rate from 10.4% to 2.8% on clean RoboTwin, from 10.9% to 2.5% on random-ized RoboTwin, and from 9.1% to 5.7% on LIBERO-Pro. Even when all consecutive calls with identical arguments are counted, regardless of whether they change the environ-ment, RL reduces repetition from 12.3% to 3.0% on clean RoboTwin, from 12.4% to 2.7% on randomized RoboTwin, and from 12.8% to 7.5% on LIBERO-Pro. 

RL completes tasks with fewer calls. We compare only initial states that both policies solve, since failed episodes mostly run until a budget is exhausted. On these states, VLM calls per episode drop from 23.3 to 15.4 on clean RoboTwin, from 21.0 to 13.1 on randomized RoboTwin, and from 28.1 to 25.1 on LIBERO-Pro (Table 4). The sav-ings come from different sources: on RoboTwin, mainly from fewer read-only queries (12.7 to 7.3 and 11.4 to 6.6), whereas on LIBERO-Pro query counts barely change and robot decisions drop from 13.1 to 11.4. RL thus shortens successful executions by issuing fewer queries on RoboTwin and by needing fewer actions on LIBERO-Pro.

When a completion check reports that the laptop is not yet open (b), SFT keeps querying and checking, whereas RL pushes the lid further until the check succeeds. 4.5. Ablations 

Hierarchical memory. We vary only the visible interac-tion history, keeping prompts, tools, budgets, and initial states fixed (Fig. 6). On LIBERO-Pro, we evaluate ini-tial states 1–3 of each task cell (240 episodes). For the RL model, hierarchical memory achieves 44.0% success on RoboTwin and 75.4% on LIBERO-Pro. Removing history reduces success to 20.4% and 0%, respectively, while a re-cent window without summaries or a failure record reaches 42.0% and 41.7%. Full raw history yields only 28.0% suc-cess on RoboTwin and 54.2% on LIBERO-Pro. Even with ORA’s built-in compression, full history uses roughly twice as many prompt tokens per call as hierarchical memory, yet achieves lower success rates: 40.8% versus 44.0% on RoboTwin and 70.4% versus 75.4% on LIBERO-Pro. Since the RL policy is trained with hierarchical mem-ory, its performance may partly reflect familiarity with this memory setting. To examine whether the benefit extends to a model without such training, we also evaluate the base Qwen3.5-9B on RoboTwin. Hierarchical memory achieves 20.0% success, compared with 12.8% without history and 8.4% with the full raw history. This consistent advantage suggests that the benefit is not solely due to training with hierarchical memory. 

These context savings also improve execution and training efficiency. Hierarchical memory uses about half the prompt tokens per call of the compressed full history (28.4K vs. 53.7K on RoboTwin and 24.4K vs. 53.1K on LIBERO-Pro) and 23% less time per episode on RoboTwin. On the same three RoboTwin task groups, one policy update under the compressed full history takes 1.65 × the GPU time of hierarchical memory (4.00 vs. 2.43 GPU-hours). ## 5. Conclusion 

RoboHarness-RL trains VLMs for tool-based manipulation through agentic RL, with LiteHarness supporting efficient multi-turn interaction. RL improves success beyond teacher imitation without VLAs or WAMs, reduces repetitive calls, shortens successful executions, and improves recovery from negative feedback. Gains extend to randomized scenes un-seen during training. Randomized bimanual manipulation and long-horizon tasks remain challenging; transfer to real robots remains to be evaluated.

A.6. Statistical Significance 

Because SFT and RL are evaluated on identical task–initial-state pairs, we compare them with paired tests (Table A4). For success rates, we report 95% Wilson intervals for each policy and a 95% interval for the RL gain from a bootstrap that resamples tasks (10,000 resamples), which accounts for the correlation among episodes of the same task. The p-values come from exact two-sided McNemar tests on the discordant pairs. The RL gain is significant on all three eval-uations, and every bootstrap interval for the gain excludes zero. The behavioral findings in Sec. 4.4 are tested with exact two-sided sign tests over paired episodes. Per-episode rates of repetitive calls, as defined in Sec. 4.4, are lower after RL in all three settings ( p = 3 ×10 −7 and 4×10 −6 on clean and randomized RoboTwin, and p = 0 .013 on LIBERO-Pro). Counting all identical consecutive calls gives p < 10 −7 on both RoboTwin conditions and p < 10 −16 on LIBERO-Pro. for clean RoboTwin, randomized RoboTwin, and LIBERO-Pro). ## B. LiteHarness 

B.1. Tool Interface 

LiteHarness keeps the tools of ORA [61] and their argu-ment schemas unchanged. Table A5 lists the 20 tools available on RoboTwin. SAM 3 [3] serves text ground-ing (vision ground ) and point-prompted segmenta-tion ( vision segment ). Contact-GraspNet [48] pro-poses grasps for grasp plan , combined on RoboTwin with a top-down grasp heuristic for the parallel-jaw grip-pers. Motion targets are planned with cuRobo [47], whereas align plan solves inverse kinematics only. On LIBERO-Pro, the single-arm embodiment exposes 18 tools; 

grasp execute and align plan are absent, and mo-tion tools take their targets without an arm argument. Tools are either read-only or world-changing. Read-only tools may be called several times or batched within one re-sponse. A world-changing tool must be the only call in its response and ends the turn, after which the next observa-tion shows its effect. If a response mixes the two kinds, the read-only calls are executed and the world-changing call is rejected. Each executed world-changing call counts toward the robot-action budget Bact , and every model call counts toward Bcall .

B.2. Prompts and Tool Descriptions 

The student’s system prompt and rules are shown in Fig. A1. The system prompt is shared by both benchmarks. The rules differ only in the embodiment paragraph and in one recovery hint: the LIBERO-Pro version describes the single Franka Panda arm, its controlled point, the agentview 

and wrist cameras, and the command workspace, and it re-places the hint about moving the other arm with one about lifting after the jaw closes on an object. Teacher and student share tool names and argument schemas but receive different descriptions. The teacher sees ORA’s full descriptions, while the student receives concise rewrites that keep the argument conventions, return fields, and common refusal reasons (Fig. A2). On RoboTwin, the tool descriptions total 65.0K characters for the teacher and 16.7K for the student; on LIBERO-Pro, 55.3K and 15.1K. Parameter descriptions in the student’s schemas are short-ened to at most 100 characters, while names, types, enumer-ations, and required fields are unchanged. The first model call of an episode contains no interaction history. In an ex-ample episode, this first call contains about 31K prompt to-kens under ORA and 12.5K under LiteHarness. Rules (RoboTwin). Evidence: a measurement or candidate is tied to the observation it came from. After any motion, geometry that could have moved is stale until a fresh observation or a new measurement confirms it; re-ground before planning on it. When two measurements disagree by enough to change the action, take another view or measurement; never average them. Command accepted, endpoint reached and task done are three different claims; only claim what the current evidence supports. An object missing from one camera may be out of its field or occluded: change the viewpoint instead of assuming it is gone. Depth-based world points come from the localization tools; calibration alone gives a ray. 

When something fails: a refusal, an error or an unchanged world says the approach was wrong, not only the tool. Before repeating, change what the attempt rests on: refresh the evidence the arguments came from, or take a different route to the same effect. Two failures of the same kind mean the premise is wrong. A failed attempt rules out that pose, contact or route, not the object or the goal. Typical recoveries: a stale candidate or reference →

run grasp plan or vision ground again on the current observation; unreachable or planner failed → pick another candidate, use the reported reachable fraction or nearest reachable orientation, or lift first and move in a shorter segment; blocked by contact → back off along the approach direction and come in from another side or with the other arm; inter-arm collision → move the other arm away first. 

Boundary: the task sentence (and its public contract, when given) is the objective. Simulator state, segmentation truth, contacts and the success predicate are private. Only the evaluator’s verdict at the end counts; motion completion is not success. Measured state can change without a command (settling, gravity).

B.4. Hierarchical Memory 

LiteHarness builds each context from the recorded interac-tion by deterministic rules, without additional model calls. The same rules render teacher recordings into student con-texts for SFT. 

Recent memory Rt. The window contains the current step and the K = 3 preceding steps, where a step is the interaction between two consecutive observations. Each ob-servation’s text body is shortened to at most 2,500 charac-ters; image retention follows the separate rule below. Robot actions and their results are kept, with numbers rounded to four decimals. Within each step, the last two read-only queries are kept exactly. Earlier queries are compressed: the model’s text and the call arguments are shortened to at most 300 characters, and each result is replaced by a di-gest of at most 900 characters. The digest keeps the fields needed for later tool use, including status and failure codes, candidate IDs and target references, scores, 3D centers and extents, bounding boxes, support heights, positions, orien-tations, and task success, with at most eight items per list. 

Historical summaries St. Steps older than the window are reduced to their action. The observation is replaced by a single line naming the last action and its outcome, the action call is kept with its arguments shortened to 300 characters, and its result is replaced by a digest. Read-only queries of these steps are removed. 

Failure record Ft. The failure record lists every failed action and every rejected call from completed steps, with the step index, the tool, compressed arguments, and the re-ported cause. It keeps the 20 most recent entries and is placed immediately before the steps shown in full. Fig-ure A3 shows the formats of the step summaries and the failure record. 

Images. Images are retained only from the current step and the two preceding steps, a shorter window than the tex-tual recent memory. Images from older steps are omitted even when their text remains in Rt. Images returned by tools before the current observation are replaced by a place-holder, so tool-generated previews do not persist across steps. If more than Bimg = 24 images remain, whole mes-sages lose their images, oldest first, until at most 24 remain. 

Teacher view. The teacher and student use the same memory rules, including the recent window, historical sum-maries, failure record, observation limits, and image cap. The teacher receives full tool descriptions, while the stu-dent uses concise descriptions. 22 C. Teacher Demonstrations and Supervised Fine-Tuning 

C.1. Teacher Data Collection 

The teacher is GPT-6 Astra, accessed through an OpenAI-compatible API with high reasoning effort and the provider’s default sampling. It acts in the same harness as the student and follows the same memory rules, while re-ceiving full tool descriptions as detailed in Appendix B.4. Teacher episodes use ORA’s default budgets of 200 model calls and 80 robot decisions. On RoboTwin, demonstrations are collected in clean scenes on qualified seeds that are disjoint from the eval-uation seeds and from the RL seed pool (Appendix A.1). On LIBERO-Pro, they are collected on initial states 11– 49 of all 80 task cells, adding initial states until a cell has ten successful demonstrations. Among teacher episodes that ran to completion, 88.7% succeed on RoboTwin and 88.1% on LIBERO-Pro; the remaining episodes end with the teacher giving up or exhausting the robot-action budget. On LIBERO-Pro, success is lower in later batches, which target the cells that remained short of ten demonstrations. Because the teacher is served through an external API, we monitored each collection batch for changes in its be-havior. For every batch, we tracked the success rate, the mean numbers of model calls and robot decisions per episode, and the change in success between the first and sec-ond halves of the batch. A batch was stopped and inspected when any of these left its reference range, for example when RoboTwin success fell below 70% or the mean number of model calls exceeded 60 per episode. 

C.2. From Teacher Episodes to SFT Samples 

Only episodes that pass the official success check are used. Successful episodes with more than 80 model calls on RoboTwin, or more than 120 on LIBERO-Pro, are dis-carded, as such successes are typically reached only af-ter extended unproductive interaction; this removes 11 and 2 episodes, respectively. On RoboTwin, eight further episodes are held out for validation, leaving the 393 train-ing episodes reported in Sec. 4.1. Each remaining model call yields one training sample. The input is the context the student would receive at that call, produced by applying the deterministic LiteHarness rendering of Appendix B.4 to the recorded teacher request, and the target is the teacher’s re-sponse, including its text and tool calls. Three kinds of calls are excluded as targets: responses rejected by the harness, calls whose tool result was a refusal, and responses con-taining neither text nor a tool call. Excluding refused calls prevents the student from imitating invalid arguments, while the teacher’s corrected call that follows is kept. Images are resized to at most 81,920 pixels, matching the resolution at which the inference server processes them. Each sample is rendered with the student’s chat template before training to confirm that it matches the input the student receives during evaluation. Table A6 summarizes the resulting datasets. C.3. Supervised Fine-Tuning 

SFT applies LoRA with rank 16, α = 32 , and dropout 0.05 to all linear projections of the language model, includ-ing the attention, MLP, and linear-attention projections of Qwen3.5; the vision encoder is frozen. D.2. Reward and Advantage 

Reward. The reward of episode i is its official success, reduced by a bounded penalty for stalling after a failed com-pletion check:

We use λ = 0 .5 and c = 160 , so the penalty never exceeds half of the success reward and successful episodes always rank above failed ones within a group. The penalty targets the most common failure of the SFT policies: af-ter a negative check, the policy keeps querying without act-ing until the model-call budget is exhausted. Such episodes frequently form groups in which every rollout fails, which yield no gradient under success alone; the penalty separates failures that keep acting from failures that stall. Groups whose rewards span less than 0.05 are treated as uninfor-mative and excluded from the update, so a group of fail-ures enters training only when the stall penalty separates its episodes noticeably.

D.5. Compute 

Each RL round runs on a single node with eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs, alternating between rollout collection and the policy update on the same GPUs. During collection on RoboTwin, the policy is served by vLLM on three GPUs, one GPU hosts eight instances each of SAM 3 and Contact-GraspNet, and six GPUs run the simulators with eight episodes each, giving 48 parallel episodes; two of the vLLM GPUs also run simulators. LIBERO-Pro uses two GPUs for vLLM and runs twelve episodes per simulator GPU from round 3, giving 72 parallel episodes. The update uses verl with FSDP2 on all eight GPUs. SFT runs data-parallel on the same type of node.

Training cost of memory. To measure how memory af-fects training cost, we take the round-25 RoboTwin pol-icy and the next three task groups in its schedule (lift pot, shake bottle, and stack blocks two; eight rollouts each). For hierarchical memory, we use the 24 rollouts recorded during training; for ORA’s compressed full history, we sam-ple 24 new rollouts from the same policy on the same groups. We then run one complete update, scoring and gradient step, on the same eight-GPU node and record to-kens and time per batch; the updated weights are discarded. The full-history rows require raising the prompt limit from 45,056 to 77,824 tokens, and 10% of them still exceed it (Table A10). Over the prompts of the 250 clean evalua-tion episodes, 76% of full-history calls exceed 45,056 to-kens, whereas no hierarchical row is dropped in the update. Per million tokens, the longer sequences cost only 3–12% more, so most of the difference comes from the 1.5 × as many tokens entering the update. Since the update accounts for more than two thirds of each round, a complete round would cost about 1.45 × as much under the compressed full history. This is a single measurement on RoboTwin used only for timing; it does not compare the success of policies trained under each memory. 

D.6. Training Dynamics 

Figure A6 shows the training statistics of every round. Roll-out success is measured on the training pairs of each round, which change with the schedule, so it fluctuates more than evaluation success. On LIBERO-Pro, it rises from 31% over the first three rounds to 65% over the last five, although the later stages concentrate on difficult cells; on RoboTwin, it rises from 28% to 38% over the same spans. Each round yields 7.4 informative groups out of 12 on RoboTwin and 8.6 on LIBERO-Pro on average, and every round contains at least four. Episodes become shorter on LIBERO-Pro, from 110 to 75 model calls on average, consistent with the lower execution overhead in Sec. 4.4, while the invalid-call rate stays below 1.6% on both benchmarks. The optimization stays close to the rollout policy. Token entropy remains between 0.19 and 0.30 on RoboTwin and between 0.12 and 0.17 on LIBERO-Pro without a down-ward trend, so the policy does not collapse onto a narrow set of responses. The approximate KL divergence between consecutive policies stays below 2 × 10 −3 per round, the fraction of clipped tokens below 0.5%, and the mean impor-tance weight of the rollout correction within 10 −4 of one. Each round performs 4 to 16 optimizer updates, depending on the number of rows in informative groups.

E.3. Where RL Episodes Still Fail 

On LIBERO-Pro, we categorize failed episodes by the fur-thest stage reached (Table A12), counting a grasp as held when the gripper does not fully close after closing on an object. After RL, nearly all failures reach the placement stage: in 88% of failed episodes the policy has grasped and placed an object, yet the official check fails, compared with 77% for SFT. Episodes that never close the gripper or never place a held object nearly disappear (58 versus 8), whereas closing without holding an object remains similar (20 ver-sus 18). On the 183 initial states that both policies fail, RL performs more pick-and-place attempts (3.2 versus 2.4 per episode, p = 8 × 10 −8) and tries more grasp locations (2.9 versus 2.2, p = 3 × 10 −7), so RL failures result from at-tempts that do not reach the required placement rather than from stopping early. Neither policy declares success prema-turely: no LIBERO-Pro episode ends with a success claim that the official check rejects.

Dependence on the teacher. We initialize RL from teacher demonstrations because the base model rarely suc-ceeds on its own: the untrained Qwen3.5-9B reaches 20.0% on clean RoboTwin and 1.8% on LIBERO-Pro, which leaves too few successful rollouts for RL to learn from. Remaining failure modes. RL improves how the policy uses the tools, while perception, grasp proposals, and mo-tion planning come from fixed external models.
