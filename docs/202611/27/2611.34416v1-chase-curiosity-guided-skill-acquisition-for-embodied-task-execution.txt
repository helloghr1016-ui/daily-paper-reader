Abstract. Robots executing tasks in partially observed environments must resolve uncertainty while operating with incomplete skills. Inspired by human curiosity, we investigate how robots can formulate task-relevant hypotheses, seek evidence, and acquire missing skills during task execution. We propose CHASE, a Curiosity-guided Harness for Acquiring Skills from Evidence, which operationalizes curiosity as selecting investigations that resolve task uncertainty and expand executable capabilities. Four modules connect dynamic scene understanding, hypothesis-driven planning, on-demand skill acquisition, and verified execution. The planner balances information value, skill reuse, and acquisition/execution cost. Missing skills trigger bounded exploration, learning from verified segments, and held-out admission during acquisition; separate checks verify physical effects and acquired evidence. Newly admitted skills expand the executable investigation set, closing the loop between resolving uncertainty and acquiring capabilities. Simulation and real-robot experiments evaluate investigation, skill acquisition, and task execution. CHASE achieves 50–80% task success under combined information and capability gaps, with 70–90% evidence resolution. “Bring me the mug.”

1 Introduction 

Humans are naturally curious. Faced with an unfamiliar situation, they formulate hypotheses, seek evidence, and learn new ways to act. This ability motivates a question for embodied intelligence: how can we endow robots with curiosity that supports task execution? A robot may know the desired outcome while lacking information about the environment or a skill needed to achieve it. We study the coupling between these information and capability gaps: useful interactions reveal what the robot needs to know, while acquiring a missing skill can make previously unavailable interactions possible. A conventional execution pipeline restricted to current observations and a fixed skill library can stall when information or an operation is missing. Figure 1 illustrates this issue through a retrieval example. The robot on the left concludes, “I can’t see it. Task over.” The robot on the right asks, “Could it be in the drawer?” It seeks evidence, recognizes the missing opening skill, and acquires that skill before retrieval. This example illustrates a broader principle: task-directed curiosity connects task hypotheses, evidence-seeking interactions, and capability expansion, so that uncertainty and missing skills become explicit planning objectives. Three research directions provide foundations. Semantic maps and scene graphs ground environmental reasoning [1–3], while interactive search reveals hidden targets [4, 5]. Generalist policies support adaptation [6, 7], and model-guided data generation supports skill learning [8, 9]. Agentic systems ground and compose operations with planning and feedback [10–13]. CHASE connects these directions through an explicit loop: unresolved questions guide investigation and skill acquisition, while newly admitted skills enable further investigations. This coupling requires distinguishing a successful physical operation from an observation that actually answers the question. We propose CHASE, a Curiosity-guided Harness for Acquiring Skills from Evidence , with four modules: dynamic scene understanding, hypothesis-driven curiosity planning, on-demand skill acquisition, and composition with verified execution. Scene memory grounds task hypotheses and retains observation support. A frozen high-level planner ranks investigations by information value, skill reuse, and total cost. Applicable skills execute directly; missing operations trigger bounded generalist exploration during acquisition. Verified segments support local-policy learning, and held-out validation determines skill admission. New skills expand the executable investigation set. Separate physical and evidence checks update the scene and hypotheses, while task-specific predicates verify completion. Held-out reuse evaluation freezes the registry. The contributions are threefold: (1) a curiosity-driven task-execution framework that jointly ranks investigations by information value, capability-expansion value, and acquisition/execution cost, coupling task questions to skill acquisition and newly enabled investigations; (2) an on-demand skill-acquisition and verification mechanism that connects generalist exploration, feedback-guided refinement, verified-trajectory learning, and held-out admission to form reusable skills, with separate physical and information checks for composition; and (3) a simulation and real-robot experimental evaluation using interactive manipulation tasks to assess task execution, skill reuse, and the coupling between investigation and capability acquisition. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Semantic Scene Understanding and Curiosity-driven Object Search 

Semantic representations connect observations to task-relevant entities and relations. Hydra con-structs and optimizes hierarchical 3D scene graphs online [14]. ConceptFusion [1] and VLMaps [15] fuse visual-language features with spatial maps, enabling open-set queries and language-grounded navigation. ConceptGraphs represents objects and their relations in an open-vocabulary graph [2]; DovSG extends scene-graph reasoning to dynamic updates for long-term mobile manipulation [3]. These representations provide a basis for grounding task hypotheses and preserving observations across interactions.

Finding a hidden target additionally requires deciding what to observe or change. Active perception treats sensing as a controlled process [16], while prediction-based intrinsic rewards encourage exploration [17]. Goal-oriented semantic exploration [18] and VLFM [19] use semantic information to guide search. Semantic Mechanical Search [4] and HIMOS [5] incorporate physical interactions with occluders or containers; Explore until Confident uses confidence-calibrated exploration for question answering [20]. CHASE builds on these ideas by coupling evidence-seeking requests to capability availability: an informative investigation can trigger acquisition of its missing operation. Physical progress and the evidence needed to resolve a hypothesis are checked separately. 

2.2 Autonomous Robot Skill Acquisition and Adaptation 

Large-scale robot learning supplies increasingly versatile starting capabilities. RT-1 [21] learns language-conditioned control from diverse demonstrations, and RT-2 transfers vision-language knowledge into actions [22]. Open X-Embodiment studies cross-robot data and policy transfer [23]; Octo [6] and OpenVLA [7] provide adaptable generalist policies. Such models offer execution or adaptation backends, while deciding which missing operation to acquire remains a task-level decision. Foundation models can also guide skill discovery and data generation. MOKA uses mark-based visual prompting for manipulation [24]; Scaling Up and Distilling Down combines language-guided data collection with policy learning [8]. RoboGen generates tasks, environments, and training supervision [9], while Eureka iteratively improves reward programs [25]. RoboCat improves manipulation using demonstrations and self-generated experience [26], and Voyager accumulates executable skills in Minecraft [27]. CHASE targets task-triggered capability gaps: bounded generalist attempts produce operation traces, independently verified segments support policy training, and held-out execution determines admission. Diffusion Policy [28] provides an established learning backend; our focus is the acquisition interface and its feedback to investigation planning. 

2.3 Agent-based Skill Composition and Long-horizon Mobile Manipulation 

Language-model agents connect instructions to executable operations. SayCan grounds skill selection in affordances [10]; Code as Policies [29] and ProgPrompt [30] generate programs over available interfaces. VoxPoser composes spatial value maps for manipulation [31], and PaLM-E integrates embodied observations with language reasoning [32]. SayPlan uses hierarchical scene graphs and replanning to scale task planning [11]; Inner Monologue incorporates execution feedback [12]. Harness VLA orchestrates a frozen contact executor with memory and a fixed primitive library [13]. Mobile manipulation further couples semantic decisions with navigation and physical execution. TidyBot combines preference reasoning with household cleanup [33]; Mobile ALOHA learns bimanual whole-body behaviors from teleoperation [34]. AutoRT uses foundation models to orchestrate robot data collection [35]. These systems motivate composition, feedback, and reusable interfaces across extended tasks. CHASE investigates a complementary coupling: the agent schedules investigations and existing skills, requests acquisition when capability is insufficient, and revisits pending requests after admission. Our comparisons therefore share initial capabilities and budgets, separating orchestration choices from changes in the skill registry.

3.2 Stage 1: Dynamic 3D Scene Understanding 

Following DovSG [3], posed RGB-D observations support open-vocabulary instance association, three-dimensional geometry fusion, spatial relations, and local updates. We represent Gt = ( Vt, Rt),where nodes describe objects, containers, and observed regions, and edges encode relations such as 

on , inside , and near . New views are aligned to the existing map before association and fusion. Interactions trigger updates to affected instances and relations, preserving unrelated scene memory. We augment this representation with articulation estimates, visible support, observation history, candidate interaction attributes, and reachability evidence. These are task-specific extensions; an object label alone does not establish that it is openable or reachable. Geometry and kinematics provide candidate checks, while execution supplies physical verification. Each attribute retains its observation source, camera pose, timestamp, and validity scope. Moving an occluder invalidates dependent visibility claims; opening a different drawer need not invalidate an already observed surface.

4.1.3 Acquisition, admission, and reuse 

Acquisition starts from S0 and permits bounded exploration, learning, and registry updates. Admission and frozen-policy reuse use disjoint configurations with changed layouts, unseen objects, or combined shifts; scene and evidence updates remain enabled. Baselines use matched initial observations, initial capabilities, permitted APIs, and per-stage resource limits; all attempts, validation executions, and failed candidates enter acquisition cost.

4.3 Hypothesis-Driven Investigation 

Q1 links a hypothesis, investigation, observation, and evidence update. With fixed skills, hypothesis-driven search achieves 65–85% T1 success, compared with 20–40% for reactive execution. This comparison supports explicit investigation when capabilities are sufficient. In T3, removing information ranking changes evidence resolution from 70–90% to 60–85% (Table 4), linking investigation selection to supported answers. Figure 3 illustrates this process: nondetection motivates further investigation, exposed patches provide local evidence, and an under-bed observation supports phone localization before retrieval. A clear patch does not exclude a whole occluded region. False exclusion means ruling out a region without adequate evidence; false completion means declaring success with an unsatisfied terminal predicate. Absent-target episodes are scored separately from positive retrieval.

Resets and interventions are distinguished from autonomous completion. 

4.7 Failure Modes and Scope 

The ablations identify sensitivity to several failure mechanisms. Removing applicability gating gives 35–70% T3 success versus 50–80% for CHASE, consistent with the importance of matching a skill to supported conditions. Merged checks reduce the reported evidence-resolution range, showing why physical progress alone is insufficient to establish an answer. Failure attribution distinguishes unsupported hypotheses, invalid evidence updates, missing or misapplied skills, execution failures, and terminal-verification errors. A failed opening leaves an operation gap; an opened but unobserved interior leaves an information gap. Correct localization followed by a failed grasp remains a manipulation failure, and possession without released delivery does not satisfy retrieval. These distinctions connect investigation, acquisition, and verification to separate stages of task completion. Conclusions concern the evaluated scenes, skill scopes, and budgets; physical search and inspection do not by themselves establish broader manipulation coverage. 

5 Conclusion 

CHASE couples evidence-seeking investigation, reusable skill acquisition, and verified task com-position. Fixed-library search supports T1 investigation, while CHASE achieves 60–85% success in T2 and 50–80% in T3 when capabilities are incomplete. Frozen reuse reaches at least 70% for all acquired operations, and separate outcome checks support 70–90% evidence resolution in T3. Together, these results connect capability formation to investigation and task execution. Physical search and inspection achieve 55–85% success across three task types. Remaining challenges include reliable evidence under incomplete visibility, acquisition-cost estimation, and validation across broader objects and environments.
